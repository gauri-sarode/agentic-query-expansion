\begin{abstract}
Large language model (LLM) query expansion is typically applied either
unconditionally or not at all, despite evidence that unconditional
expansion is net-harmful on a non-trivial fraction of queries. We
formulate query expansion as a bounded, observable agentic decision,
evaluated under a strictly separated validation/test protocol: every
retrieval-config, verifier-model, and threshold decision is fixed on
TripClick TAIL \emph{validation} before a single frozen touch of
\emph{test}. An under-tuned BM25 baseline (nDCG@10 $0.228$), corrected
via validation-only stemming and title weighting, reaches $0.315$,
above the published reference, changing every downstream conclusion.
Pre-action telemetry predicts whether retrieval is already poor with
ROC-AUC $0.649$, but the same telemetry is markedly worse at the
harder, actionable question --- will a \emph{specific} expansion help
--- at ROC-AUC only $0.552$ ($r=0.114$, $p<0.001$). Detection is not
actionability, and that gap is this paper's central finding.
Consistent with it, neither static expansion nor a closed-loop agent
significantly outperforms corrected BM25 (static: $+0.0007$, agent:
$+0.0009$ nDCG@10), nor does the agent beat static; decomposing the
pipeline shows reranking and expansion are each significantly
\emph{harmful} alone but interact positively together, not one
canceling a fixed cost from the other. This is not for lack of
headroom: an oracle gains $+0.0246$ nDCG@10 over BM25 ($p<0.001$), of
which the verifier captures only $0.5$--$12\%$ across intervention
budgets. Fault-injection recovery is fault-specific, causally
explained by which telemetry features each fault perturbs --- one
fault even \emph{increases} the verifier's healthy-state score rather
than merely evading detection. A diagnostic rule built from three
faults correctly anticipated a fourth, held-out fault's
detection/false-alarm regime before it was run, though whether
detection converts to a significant recovery gain remains fault-specific
(confirmed for only one of four). Generalization is worse outside the
calibration split: action-utility AUC falls to $0.148$ (HEAD) and
$0.214$ (TORSO), below chance. We report a real implementation defect
found mid-study and its correction. Runtime observability reliably
detects retrieval failure and, for large structured faults, indicates
in advance how the verifier will behave toward a new failure class; but
the stronger property general autonomous recovery needs --- predicting
whether a specific intervention on an ordinary query will help --- is
not delivered by the same telemetry, under a protocol designed so
these findings are trustworthy.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Large language models can generate useful pseudo-documents, alternative
phrasings, and disambiguating context for a search query
\cite{query2doc,mugi}, and corpus-grounded generation --- conditioning
the rewrite on retrieved evidence rather than the query text alone ---
improves on naive query-only rewriting. Existing work establishes that
LLM query expansion \emph{can} help, not when an operational system
should trust an expansion enough to use it. In practice, expansion is
applied unconditionally or not at all; neither is safe, since
unconditional rewriting is reliably net-harmful on a measurable
fraction of queries, particularly underspecified or already-well-served
ones.

We treat this as a control problem rather than a generation problem.
Borrowing the observe-diagnose-act-recover discipline of site
reliability engineering \cite{sre-slo} and the trace/metric vocabulary
of OpenTelemetry \cite{opentelemetry}, we build an agent
whose action space is a small set of interpretable, corpus-grounded
expansion operators, whose control loop alternates actions with
structured observations as in ReAct \cite{react}, and whose accept,
roll-back, replan, and abstain decisions are driven by runtime
telemetry rather than the language model's own self-assessment. The
central question is not whether an LLM can produce a useful
expansion --- that is established --- but whether \emph{runtime
observability} can make expansion selective, recoverable, and
cost-aware. Answering it surfaces a distinction the selective query
expansion and query performance prediction literature has not, to our
knowledge, isolated: predicting that retrieval is failing and
predicting that a specific intervention will fix it are different
tasks, and telemetry good at the first can be useless at the second.
This paper reports what we found, including where the answer was no.

\subsection{Research Questions}

\textbf{RQ1 (Detection vs.\ Actionability).} Can qrel-free retrieval
and agent-runtime telemetry (a) identify that unexpanded retrieval is
already unhealthy, and (b) predict whether a \emph{specific} expansion
action is likely to help or harm --- and, if both are possible, is the
second any easier than the first once the first is solved?

\textbf{RQ2 (Control).} Does an agent that conditions expansion actions
on that telemetry outperform an otherwise identical \emph{static},
failure-conditioned pipeline --- same retriever, grounding, generator,
and fusion, differing only in whether a closed loop can observe an
action's consequences and recover from a bad one?

\textbf{RQ3 (Recovery).} After a harmful rewrite or a simulated
system-level fault, can observability-driven verification detect the
degradation and select a recovery action to restore retrieval quality,
under a bounded budget --- even for a verifier whose general-purpose
detection power (RQ1) is only weakly informative?

\subsection{Contributions}

We contribute (1) an agent architecture treating LLM query expansion as
a bounded, typed-tool action inside a closed observability loop, under
a frozen train/validation/test protocol (Section~\ref{sec:protocol})
where every retrieval-config, verifier, and threshold decision is made
on validation before one single-touch test evaluation; (2) a corrected
BM25 baseline, selected entirely on validation, moving our TripClick
TAIL retriever above the published reference and changing every
downstream conclusion; (3) a direct comparison, from one telemetry
vocabulary, of failure-\emph{detection} against
intervention-\emph{utility} prediction, showing these differ and only
the first is reliable; (4) a frozen-test evaluation of the closed-loop
agent against a matched static comparator, with an oracle and
selectivity-frontier analysis quantifying captured headroom; (5) a
fault-injection study with a causal feature-perturbation analysis and a
diagnostic rule, confirmed \emph{prospectively} on a held-out fault
never used to derive it --- telemetry can indicate whether a new
failure class will be over-flagged, under-flagged, or genuinely
discriminable before it is ever observed, not just explain failures
already seen; (6) a cross-split check showing that
ranking degrades below chance outside its calibration split; and (7)
an honest account of a real implementation defect, corrected mid-study.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{LLM query expansion.} Query2doc \cite{query2doc} generates
pseudo-documents from an LLM and appends them to the query; MuGI
\cite{mugi} generates and fuses multiple expansions. Both treat
expansion as unconditional, one-shot generation, conditioning on
neither retrieval-state telemetry nor a harmful rewrite's recovery.

\textbf{Agentic control and observability.} ReAct \cite{react}
interleaves reasoning with actions and observations, the template for
our observe-act-observe structure, but with a much smaller action
space: typed retrieval operators, keeping the policy interpretable
under a non-LLM controller. OpenTelemetry's trace/metric/log
separation and GenAI semantic conventions \cite{opentelemetry} supply
a naming discipline for our telemetry; Google's SRE framing of an SLO
as a target range, not a metric to optimize \cite{sre-slo}, motivates
our Agent Search SLO: quality subject to harm, cost, and latency
frozen \emph{before} evaluation.

\textbf{Query performance prediction and selective expansion.} Classic
query performance prediction \cite{qpp-clarity} and selective query
expansion \cite{selective-qe} already ask whether a query is hard, or
likely to benefit from expansion, from retrieval signal alone. We
split that into two predictive targets sharing one telemetry
vocabulary --- is retrieval failing, versus will \emph{this} action
fix it --- and show qrel-free telemetry answers the first far better
(Section~\ref{sec:rq1}).

\textbf{Benchmarks.} TripClick \cite{tripclick} has genuine
head/torso/tail query-frequency structure, unlike benchmarks that treat
``hard'' as a synonym for low-BM25-score, which is why it is our sole
evaluation corpus.

\section{System Design}

\subsection{Agent State and Control Loop}

The agent's state at step $t$ is $s_t = (q_0, q_t, R_t, O_t, H_t, B_t)$,
where $q_0$ is the immutable original query, $q_t$ the active search
expression, $R_t$ the current ranking, $O_t$ the observability state,
$H_t$ the episode's action/observation history, and $B_t$ the remaining
action budget. The control loop is $\textsc{Observe} \to
\textsc{Diagnose} \to \textsc{Act} \to \textsc{Retrieve} \to
\textsc{Observe} \to \textsc{Verify} \to \{\textsc{Accept},
\textsc{Rollback}, \textsc{Replan}, \textsc{Stop}\}$. The critical property this loop is built to test is that the second
decision (accept/rollback/replan/stop) depends on evidence the agent's
own first action created --- as opposed to a static pipeline that
receives the same query, initial ranking, evidence, generator, and
verifier, but executes exactly one predetermined route. Isolating this
dependency, not simply comparing against unmodified BM25, is the
paper's central experimental contrast (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}).

The action space is deliberately small and interpretable: $A=\{$
\textsc{NoExpand}, \textsc{Vocabulary}, \textsc{Context},
\textsc{Ambiguity}, \textsc{EntityNormalize}, \textsc{Rollback},
\textsc{Replan}, \textsc{Stop} $\}$.
\textsc{Vocabulary} maps informal user language onto
corpus-compatible terminology; \textsc{Context} adds a corpus-supported
missing facet to an underspecified query; \textsc{Ambiguity}
disambiguates toward the interpretation the evidence best supports;
\textsc{EntityNormalize} resolves aliases, abbreviations, and
canonical-name mismatches. The LLM is invoked through exactly one typed
tool, \texttt{generate\_expansion(action, query, evidence)}; it is
never the sole arbiter of its own output's use --- that judgment is a
separate, non-generative controller's (Section~\ref{sec:controller}).

\subsection{Observability Model}
\label{sec:observability}

Every query episode produces one trace with spans
\texttt{retrieve\_original} $\to$ \texttt{build\_evidence} $\to$
\texttt{plan\_action} $\to$ \texttt{generate\_expansion} $\to$
\texttt{retrieve\_expanded} $\to$ \texttt{rerank} $\to$ \texttt{verify}
$\to$ \texttt{recover\_or\_accept} $\to$ \texttt{fuse}. $O_t$ spans
three telemetry families. \emph{Retrieval}: top score, score margin,
normalized score entropy, query-term coverage, mean rare-term IDF,
entity overlap, top-$k$ ranking overlap against the previous state,
reranker/BM25 disagreement, lexical result coherence, and ranking
stability under a small controlled query perturbation. \emph{Agent}:
selected operator, trajectory length, previous verifier score,
rewrite-to-original semantic drift, introduced entities, repeated
actions, remaining budget. \emph{System}: per-tool latency, token
counts, tool errors/timeouts. Every feature is computable without
relevance judgments, which is what makes the verifier usable at
inference time.

\subsection{Failure Taxonomy}

We label episodes into ten failure categories (vocabulary mismatch,
underspecification, ambiguity, entity mismatch, intent drift,
confirmation bias, retriever disagreement, tool degradation, budget
exhaustion, healthy retrieval), each with a telemetry signature and
preferred recovery action, motivating Section~\ref{sec:rq3}'s
fault-injection protocol.

\subsection{Verifier and Controller}
\label{sec:controller}

The verifier, \texttt{verify(previous\_state, new\_state)}, produces a
scalar judgment of whether an action's consequences look like an
improvement, using telemetry alone. Rather than hand-picking its
weights, we calibrate them against click-derived qrels
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}): execute one expansion action, compute the
true nDCG@10 change against those labels, and fit the verifier's
weights to predict that change from Section~\ref{sec:observability}'s
telemetry features. Click labels are available only during this
offline calibration; the deployed verifier never sees one, and is
treated as an artifact --- trained by a script, loaded at inference
time. The recovery controller is a small deterministic finite-state
machine: below a calibrated harmful threshold the agent rolls back to
the pre-action ranking; above a calibrated accept threshold it fuses
the new ranking via reciprocal-rank fusion; in between it replans with
an untried operator, capped at two model calls per query.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Datasets}

TripClick \cite{tripclick} is the primary benchmark: 1{,}523{,}878
documents, split evenly across head/torso/tail frequency buckets, each
further split into train/validation/test (1{,}175 queries per split
per bucket). All results concern the TAIL split unless stated
otherwise (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}). Labels are click-derived,
not human-assessed (binary raw clicks, matching the label type this
split's published reference point uses \cite{tripclick}); we use
``qrel'' and ``click label'' interchangeably, neither implying
editorial judgment.

\subsection{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

A first pass at this study calibrated the verifier and thresholds on
the same episodes later used to report RQ1--RQ3 --- an in-sample
characterization unsuited to an out-of-sample generalization claim. We
froze a stricter protocol instead, in a committed config rather than
left implicit: TAIL \emph{validation} may be used for any
retrieval-config, verifier, or threshold decision; TAIL \emph{test} may
be touched at most once per research question, purely for evaluation,
after every upstream decision is frozen, with no decision afterward
conditioned on a test-set outcome. This discipline directly produced
the paper's first finding, below.

\subsection{A Corrected Retrieval Baseline}
\label{sec:bm25-fix}

BM25 over an unstemmed FTS5 index scored nDCG@10 $0.228$ on TAIL test
--- $15\%$ below the $0.267$ reference for this exact split and label
type \cite{tripclick-strong-baselines}. Selecting entirely on
validation, we found two config choices that close this gap: Porter
stemming (val nDCG@10 $0.235 \to 0.270$) and weighting the title field
ten times body weight in a title/body-split FTS5 index (val nDCG@10
climbing to a clear plateau at that ratio, $0.327$, MRR peaking there
and declining beyond it). A single, one-time sanity check of the frozen
config against withheld test --- not a selection criterion --- gives
nDCG@10 $0.315$, \emph{above} the published reference. We report this
because an under-tuned baseline mechanically inflates the apparent
value of anything compared against it: every downstream conclusion in
this paper changed once this fix was in place, a precondition for the
rest of the study rather than an implementation footnote.

\subsection{Implementation}

Sparse retrieval uses BM25 over a SQLite FTS5 index \cite{sqlite-fts5}
rather than a JVM Lucene index, forced by a JVM crash under Pyserini
\cite{pyserini} on our hardware; two further silent-corruption risks
surfaced at this corpus's scale, the same category as
Section~\ref{sec:bm25-fix}'s baseline gap and Section~\ref{sec:defect}'s
telemetry defect: FTS5's default MATCH operator is \emph{conjunctive}
(collapsing recall without an error), and FTS5 indexes only the
\texttt{MATCH} column (an unindexed companion lookup degrades to a
full table scan, $18\times$ slower at $1.5$M rows). Cross-encoder
reranking (\texttt{cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2}
\cite{minilm-cross-encoder}) applies to the top 100 BM25 candidates,
reranking the top 50. Generation uses a locally hosted, 4-bit quantized
2--3B-parameter model, capped at two calls per query episode with a
32--64 token structured output, so the language model is never the
entire decision surface, only one typed tool within it.

\subsection{Verifier Calibration Methodology}
\label{sec:calib-method}

Following Section~\ref{sec:protocol}, we collect calibration episodes
(one expansion action per query, true nDCG@10 change against click
labels) on TAIL \emph{validation} ($n=1147$--$1168$), and compare
three feature families of increasing size (six retrieval-telemetry
deltas; plus four reranker/length features; plus three dense-embedding
features) against three model classes each (fixed-weight, 5-fold CV
linear, 5-fold CV GBDT), under Pearson correlation (Table~\ref{tab:rq1}),
to characterize the detection ceiling rather than accept the first
plausible fit --- never itself reported as evidence the signal
generalizes (Section~\ref{sec:rq1} does that separately, from one
frozen touch of test). Recovery thresholds are calibrated the same
way, from candidate cutoffs on validation.

\subsection{Metrics}

Retrieval quality is nDCG@10, Recall@100, and MRR, plus the fraction of
queries helped/unchanged/harmed relative to a baseline. For the
recovery study (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}) we report Detection Recall
$P(\text{intervened}\mid\text{harmful})$, False Alarm Rate
$P(\text{intervened}\mid\text{healthy})$, and recovery gain in mean
nDCG@10 with vs.\ without recovery on identically corrupted episodes.
We additionally report a retrospective \emph{oracle ceiling}: per
query, $\max(\text{baseline}, \text{treatment})$ under true click
labels, diagnosing whether headroom exists independent of whether our
qrel-free verifier can find it (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}). Significance is
via paired bootstrap (10{,}000 resamples) throughout.

\subsection{Detection Is Not Actionability}
\label{sec:detect-vs-act}

That action-utility model answers a hard question: given telemetry
before \emph{and} after a specific action, did it help? A logically
prior, cheaper question uses only pre-action telemetry: is unexpanded
retrieval already unhealthy? We build a second model from the same
eight retrieval-telemetry features, labeling a query ``poor'' when
BM25 surfaces zero relevant documents in the top 10 (test prevalence
$0.450$), selected by 5-fold CV on validation and touched once on
frozen test, identically to Section~\ref{sec:calib-method}'s protocol
--- Table~\ref{tab:rq1-test}'s last row.

The gap is large and consistent across val and test: telemetry
genuinely informative about \emph{whether} retrieval is failing
($\text{AUC}=0.649$) is markedly weaker at predicting whether a
\emph{specific candidate action fixes it} ($\text{AUC}=0.552$) ---
both frozen single-touch test numbers from the same features and
protocol, not the same result at different noise levels.
\textbf{RQ1's answer}: qrel-free telemetry reliably detects that
something is wrong, but is only weakly informative about whether a
proposed fix will work --- the gap RQ2's control result and RQ3's
fault-specific pattern trace back to.

\section{RQ3: Which Failures Are Recoverable?}
\label{sec:rq3}

RQ1 and RQ2 leave open whether observability has \emph{any} practical
value, or whether a weak general detector is useless. We test a
narrower claim: that the same weak verifier can serve as a safety net
against \emph{large, obvious} failures even though it cannot reliably
judge \emph{subtle, natural} ones. We inject three fault types at
their natural point of corruption --- an unsupported entity appended
to the expansion, a grounding passage swapped for a non-supporting
one, and the reranker disabled --- and compare, on identical corrupted
episodes, an always-accept ablation against the real controller.

\subsection{A Defect That Changed the Answer}
\label{sec:defect}

An early pass found the reranker-disabled fault essentially
undetectable (recall $0.185$) while the other two looked strong.
Tracing this revealed a real defect: the reranked ranking was
computed, at real latency cost, but never actually applied to the
ranking the agent accepted --- disabling the reranker was mechanically
a no-op regardless of verifier quality. Fixing it exposed a second,
entangled defect: the verifier's telemetry was still computed from the
pre-rerank ranking. We corrected both and re-ran calibration to
confirm the deployed verifier did not need retraining (corrected
Pearson $r=0.146$ vs.\ the original $0.134$). We report this because a
component computed purely for observability is architecturally easy to
leave disconnected from the outcome it explains, and the resulting
error looked coherent --- unrealistically strong --- until an
implausible near-zero effect prompted investigation.

The three fault types split into three distinct outcomes: no single
answer to ``is recovery useful,'' only a per-failure-type one.
\textbf{Entity injection} is detected almost every time (recall
$0.974$) but flagged almost indiscriminately (false alarm $0.939$),
netting out non-significant. \textbf{Grounding corruption} is the one
clean recovery success ($+0.0099$, $p<0.05$). \textbf{Reranker
disabled} is never detected at all (recall $0.000$): the controller
essentially always accepts.

\textbf{Why, causally.} Fitted weights alone show which features the
verifier ignores, not what a fault \emph{does} to its score:
$\Delta x_j = \mathbb{E}[x_j^{\text{fault}} - x_j^{\text{clean}}]$,
$\text{Contribution}_j = w_j \cdot \Delta x_j$ ($n{=}79$/fault;
Table~\ref{tab:rq3}, last column). The clearest case is reranker
disabled: its dominant contribution is large and \emph{positive}, from
\texttt{disagreement} moving in the direction the verifier reads as
improvement --- the fault does not merely evade weak reranker-derived
features, it makes the corrupted state score higher, so the controller
is actively misled, not
merely blind. Section~\ref{sec:diagnostic} generalizes this causal
signature into a rule and tests it prospectively.

\textbf{RQ3's answer is that recoverability is fault-specific}, and
the attribution shows why: content-level corruption produces a real
net gain only when its effect on the verifier's score is small enough
to leave room for genuine per-episode discrimination (grounding), is a
wash when the effect is large and uniform enough to flag almost
everything (entity injection), and a system-level fault is disguised
as an improvement when it moves a strongly-weighted feature the
\emph{wrong} way (reranker) --- a verifier is not uniformly weak or
strong across failure types, it can be actively adversarial to a fault
it was never calibrated against. This pattern is not just
post-hoc description, either: Section~\ref{sec:diagnostic} tests it
prospectively.

\subsection{A Diagnostic Rule, Confirmed on a Held-Out Fault}
\label{sec:diagnostic}

The three faults' net contributions ($\sum_j w_j\Delta x_j$) and their
recall/false-alarm pairs move together: strongly negative (entity
injection, $-0.0529$) pushes the score down almost regardless of true
harm, giving high recall but comparably high false alarms; strongly
positive (reranker disabled, $+0.1296$) does the opposite (recall/FA
near zero); only near-zero (grounding, $+0.0002$) leaves room for
per-episode variation to separate harmful from healthy cases --- the
one fault with a significant net gain. This is usable \emph{before} a
new fault is ever run: reason about which features a candidate
corruption should perturb, weight by the frozen verifier's
coefficients, and read off whether it will be over-flagged,
under-flagged, or genuinely discriminable.

discrimination converts into a significant recovery gain (as it did
only for grounding corruption) still requires running it, and the
per-feature mechanism does not always transfer.

\section{Discussion}

Read together, RQ1--RQ3, the oracle analysis, the diagnostic rule, and
the cross-split check sketch one finding. Section~\ref{sec:detect-vs-act}
is load-bearing: the same telemetry detects that retrieval is failing
reasonably well (AUC $0.649$) but is weakly informative about whether
a specific action on an ordinary query will fix it (AUC $0.552$). RQ2
shows the consequence --- a closed loop built on the weaker signal
adds nothing over a matched static pipeline --- and the oracle and
selectivity-frontier analyses rule out ``no opportunity exists'': real
headroom is available ($+0.0246$ nDCG@10) and the verifier reaches at
most $12\%$ of it. RQ3's causal attribution shows this is a per-fault
property, sharp enough in one case that the verifier actively reads
the fault as an improvement. The diagnostic rule
(Section~\ref{sec:diagnostic}) is the paper's sharpest positive result
precisely because it operates in this narrower regime: for
\emph{large, structured} faults, the same weights predict, before a
new fault is run, which recall/false-alarm regime it falls into.
Section~\ref{sec:robustness} bounds this differently: holding the
fault fixed and changing query frequency, the same ranking goes from
weakly informative on TAIL to below-chance on HEAD/TORSO. Two
generalization questions, opposite answers: new failure types within
the calibration split, yes; the same behavior outside it, no.

The BM25 baseline and reranker-wiring defects
(Sections~\ref{sec:bm25-fix}, \ref{sec:defect}) were both found only
by refusing a convenient result: an error that looks coherent rather
than broken. A frozen protocol with a mandatory sanity check defends
against the first kind; the second we only found by distrusting a
result that seemed too good.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The oracle ceiling (Section~\ref{sec:oracle}) is retrospective, using
test-set click labels directly --- a valid upper bound, never a
deployable method. The generator is a locally hosted, quantized 2--3B
parameter model under a two-call budget; a larger generator might
change RQ1's ceiling or RQ2's null result. Cross-split robustness
(Section~\ref{sec:robustness}) covers TripClick's three buckets only;
other benchmarks remain untested, so the AUC collapse could be
frequency-specific or reflect broader corpus-shift sensitivity.
Click-derived qrels are an imperfect relevance proxy inherited from
the benchmark. The diagnostic rule (Section~\ref{sec:diagnostic})
predicted the held-out fault's recall/false-alarm regime correctly but
not its dominant feature, and rests on one held-out fault, not a
systematic sweep. The failure-detection model uses one binary
definition of ``poor retrieval''; a graded definition could shift its
measured AUC.

\section{Conclusion}

We evaluated a closed-loop, observability-guided query-expansion agent
under a corrected protocol separating model selection from final
evaluation --- surfacing the paper's first finding: an
honestly-selected BM25 fix inverted every downstream conclusion.
Runtime observability reveals retrieval failures reliably (detection
AUC $0.649$), but autonomous recovery needs a stronger property the
same telemetry does not deliver: predicting whether a specific
intervention will help (action-utility AUC $0.552$, below chance
outside its calibration split). That gap is why the closed-loop agent
does not outperform static expansion and neither outperforms BM25,
despite real headroom going uncaptured. The recovery study shows this
is a per-fault, mechanistically explicable property --- in one case
the verifier is actively misled, not merely blind --- and, tested
prospectively on a held-out fault, this mechanism supports genuine
diagnosis in advance: predicting, before a new large structured
failure is run, whether the verifier will over-flag it, under-flag it,
or discriminate it well, though whether that translates into a
significant recovery gain still requires running it. That capability
does not extend across query-frequency regimes, where
the same ranking inverts outside its calibration split. We report both
implementation defects found by refusing a convenient result, in the
belief that the discipline to catch them is worth more than a
flattering number.
