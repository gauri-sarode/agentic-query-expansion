\title{Observe, Expand, Recover: Observability-Guided Control for Query Expansion}

\author{
\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Author(s)}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Affiliation(s) withheld for double-blind review}
}

\maketitle
\small

\begin{abstract}
LLM query expansion is typically applied unconditionally, despite
evidence it can be net-harmful on a measurable fraction of queries. We
formulate expansion as a bounded, observable agentic decision: observe
telemetry, act, verify before committing. Our central finding: qrel-free
telemetry recorded \emph{before} an action is reasonably predictive of
retrieval failure (AUC $0.649$), but recorded \emph{after} the action,
it is markedly weaker at telling whether that action helped (AUC
$0.552$). Detection is not actionability. Neither a static expansion
pipeline nor a closed-loop recovery agent beats a properly-tuned
retriever on ordinary queries, despite a retrospective oracle showing
uncaptured headroom ($+0.0246$ nDCG@10) our qrel-free verifier largely
cannot exploit. A six-fault perturbation analysis shows this gap is
per-fault, with distinct, interpretable telemetry signatures. The same
ordering replicates on an independent second corpus, BEIR Natural
Questions (failure AUC $0.670$ vs.\ action-utility AUC $0.550$, paired
difference $95\%$ CI excluding zero): knowing that retrieval has
failed is substantially easier than knowing which intervention will
fix it.
\end{abstract}

query expansion, information retrieval, agentic systems, AI observability, retrieval-augmented generation, large language models

\section{Introduction}

Large language models can generate useful pseudo-documents, alternative
phrasings, and disambiguating context for a search query
\cite{query2doc,mugi}, and corpus-grounded generation --- conditioning
the rewrite on retrieved evidence rather than the query text alone ---
improves on naive query-only rewriting. Existing work establishes that
LLM query expansion \emph{can} help, but leaves open when an
operational system should trust an expansion enough to use it, and in
practice it is applied unconditionally or not at all --- neither
provides a reliable decision rule: unconditional rewriting can be
net-harmful on a measurable fraction of queries, particularly
underspecified or already-well-served ones. Our agent alternates
actions with structured observations as in ReAct
\cite{react} (Section~\ref{sec:controller}), with accept, roll-back,
replan, and abstain decisions driven by runtime telemetry, not the
model's own self-assessment. We study this distinction explicitly:
predicting that retrieval is
failing and predicting that a specific intervention will fix it are
different tasks, and telemetry useful for the first need not be useful
for the second.

\subsection{Research Questions}

\textbf{RQ1 (Detection vs.\ Actionability).} Can qrel-free retrieval
and agent-runtime telemetry (a) identify when unexpanded retrieval is
already unhealthy, and (b) predict whether a \emph{specific} expansion
action will help or harm? How much harder is the second task than the
first?

\textbf{RQ2 (Control).} Does an agent that conditions expansion actions
on that telemetry outperform an otherwise identical \emph{static},
failure-conditioned pipeline --- same retriever, grounding, generator,
and fusion, differing only in whether a closed loop can observe an
action's consequences and recover from a bad one?

\textbf{RQ3 (Recovery).} When retrieval is corrupted by a harmful
rewrite or a simulated system-level fault, can observability-driven
verification detect the degradation and select a recovery action to
restore retrieval quality under a bounded budget --- even given RQ1's
finding that this verifier's general-purpose detection power is only
weakly informative?

\subsection{Contributions}

We contribute four findings, each under a frozen validation/test
protocol (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}) so no result is conditioned on
the split used to report it. \textbf{C1, detection vs.\ actionability}:
qrel-free runtime telemetry predicts retrieval failure substantially
better than it predicts intervention utility (AUC $0.649$ vs.\
$0.552$). \textbf{C2, consequences for agentic control}: a matched
static-vs-agent evaluation, plus an oracle and selectivity-frontier
analysis, shows substantial control headroom exists ($+0.0246$
nDCG@10) but is mostly inaccessible to the verifier ($0.5$--$12\%$
captured). \textbf{C3, fault observability}: a six-fault telemetry
perturbation analysis, including three held-out fault classes, shows
fault-specific over-flagging, discrimination, and active
misclassification --- a telemetry-only probe, without relevance
labels, characterizes whether a new fault class is over-flagged,
under-flagged, or discriminable. \textbf{C4, distribution
shift}: a TAIL-calibrated verifier's action-utility ranking becomes
anti-correlated with true utility on HEAD/TORSO (AUC falling to
$0.14$--$0.23$). Two further findings are methodological lessons
rather than headline contributions: an under-tuned BM25 baseline
inflated every downstream comparison until corrected on validation
alone (Section~\ref{sec:bm25-fix}), and a real reranker-wiring defect,
found and corrected mid-study, had been masking one fault's
recoverability (Section~\ref{sec:defect}).

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{LLM query expansion.} Query2doc \cite{query2doc} generates
pseudo-documents from an LLM and appends them to the query; MuGI
\cite{mugi} generates and fuses multiple expansions. Both treat
expansion as unconditional, one-shot generation, conditioning on
neither retrieval-state telemetry nor a harmful rewrite's recovery.

\textbf{Agentic control and observability.} ReAct \cite{react}
interleaves reasoning with actions and observations, the template for
our observe-act-observe structure, but with a much smaller action
space: typed retrieval operators under a non-LLM controller.
OpenTelemetry's trace/metric/log separation and GenAI semantic
conventions \cite{opentelemetry} supply a naming discipline for our
telemetry.

\textbf{Query performance prediction and selective expansion.} Classic
query performance prediction \cite{qpp-clarity} and selective query
expansion \cite{selective-qe} already ask whether a query is hard, or
likely to benefit from expansion, from retrieval signal alone. We
split that into two targets sharing one telemetry vocabulary --- is
retrieval failing, versus will \emph{this} action fix it --- and show
qrel-free telemetry answers the first far better (Section~\ref{sec:rq1}).

\textbf{Benchmarks.} TripClick \cite{tripclick} has genuine
head/torso/tail frequency structure, unlike benchmarks that treat
``hard'' as low-BM25-score --- our reason for it as primary corpus.

\section{System Design}

\subsection{Agent State and Control Loop}

We use ``agentic'' operationally: a bounded system that observes
state, selects actions, observes consequences, and adapts under a
budget --- not a claim of open-ended autonomy. The agent's state at
step $t$ is
$s_t = (q_0, q_t, R_t, O_t, H_t, B_t)$,
where $q_0$ is the immutable original query, $q_t$ the active search
expression, $R_t$ the current ranking, $O_t$ the observability state,
$H_t$ the episode's action/observation history, and $B_t$ the remaining
action budget. The control loop is $\textsc{Observe} \to
\textsc{Diagnose} \to \textsc{Act} \to \textsc{Retrieve} \to
\textsc{Observe} \to \textsc{Verify} \to \{\textsc{Accept},
\textsc{Rollback}, \textsc{Replan}, \textsc{Stop}\}$. The critical property this loop is built to test is that the second
decision (accept/rollback/replan/stop) depends on evidence the agent's
own first action created --- as opposed to a static pipeline that
receives the same query, initial ranking, evidence, generator, and
verifier, but executes exactly one predetermined route. Isolating this
dependency, not simply comparing against unmodified BM25, is the
paper's central experimental contrast (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}).

The action space is deliberately small and interpretable: $A=\{$
\textsc{NoExpand}, \textsc{Vocabulary}, \textsc{Context},
\textsc{Ambiguity}, \textsc{EntityNormalize}, \textsc{Rollback},
\textsc{Replan}, \textsc{Stop} $\}$.
\textsc{Vocabulary} maps informal user language onto
corpus-compatible terminology; \textsc{Context} adds a corpus-supported
missing facet to an underspecified query; \textsc{Ambiguity}
disambiguates toward the interpretation the evidence best supports;
\textsc{EntityNormalize} resolves aliases, abbreviations, and
canonical-name mismatches. The LLM is invoked through exactly one typed
tool, \texttt{generate\_expansion(action, query, evidence)}; it is
never the sole arbiter of its own output's use --- that judgment is a
separate, non-generative controller's (Section~\ref{sec:controller}).

\subsection{Observability Model}
\label{sec:observability}

Every query episode produces one trace with spans
\texttt{retrieve\_original} $\to$ \texttt{build\_evidence} $\to$
\texttt{plan\_action} $\to$ \texttt{generate\_expansion} $\to$
\texttt{retrieve\_expanded} $\to$ \texttt{rerank} $\to$ \texttt{verify}
$\to$ \texttt{recover\_or\_accept} $\to$ \texttt{fuse}. $O_t$ spans
three telemetry families. \emph{Retrieval}: top score, score margin,
normalized score entropy, query-term coverage, mean rare-term IDF,
entity overlap, top-$k$ ranking overlap against the previous state,
reranker/BM25 disagreement, lexical result coherence, and ranking
stability under a small controlled query perturbation. \emph{Agent}:
selected operator, trajectory length, previous verifier score,
rewrite-to-original semantic drift, introduced entities, repeated
actions, remaining budget. \emph{System}: per-tool latency, token
counts, tool errors/timeouts. Every feature is computable without
relevance judgments, which is what makes the verifier usable at
inference time.

\subsection{Failure Taxonomy}

We label episodes into ten failure categories (vocabulary mismatch,
underspecification, ambiguity, entity mismatch, intent drift,
confirmation bias, retriever disagreement, tool degradation, budget
exhaustion, healthy retrieval), each with a telemetry signature and
preferred recovery action, motivating Section~\ref{sec:rq3}'s
fault-injection protocol.

\subsection{Verifier and Controller}
\label{sec:controller}

The verifier, \texttt{verify(previous\_state, new\_state)}, produces a
scalar judgment of whether an action's consequences look like an
improvement, using telemetry alone. Rather than hand-picking its
weights, we calibrate them against click-derived qrels
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}): execute one expansion action, compute the
true nDCG@10 change against those labels, and fit the verifier's
weights to predict that change from Section~\ref{sec:observability}'s
telemetry features. Click labels are available only during this
offline calibration; the deployed verifier never sees one, and is
treated as an artifact --- trained by a script, loaded at inference
time. The recovery controller is a small deterministic finite-state
machine: below a calibrated harmful threshold the agent rolls back to
the pre-action ranking; above a calibrated accept threshold it fuses
the new ranking via reciprocal-rank fusion; in between it replans with
an untried operator, capped at two model calls per query.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Datasets}

TripClick \cite{tripclick} is the primary benchmark: 1{,}523{,}878
documents, split evenly across head/torso/tail frequency buckets, each
further split into train/validation/test (1{,}175 queries per split
per bucket). All results concern the TAIL split unless stated
otherwise (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}). Labels are click-derived,
not human-assessed (binary raw clicks, matching the label type this
split's published reference point uses \cite{tripclick}); we use
``qrel'' and ``click label'' interchangeably, neither implying
editorial judgment.

\subsection{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

A first pass at this study calibrated the verifier and thresholds on
the same episodes later used to report RQ1--RQ3 --- an in-sample
characterization unsuited to an out-of-sample generalization claim. We
froze a stricter protocol instead, in a committed config rather than
left implicit: TAIL \emph{validation} may be used for any
retrieval-config, verifier, or threshold decision; TAIL \emph{test} may
be touched at most once per research question, purely for evaluation,
after every upstream decision is frozen, with no decision afterward
conditioned on a test-set outcome. This discipline directly produced
the paper's first finding, below.

\subsection{A Corrected Retrieval Baseline}
\label{sec:bm25-fix}

BM25 over an unstemmed FTS5 index scored nDCG@10 $0.228$ on TAIL test
--- $15\%$ below the $0.267$ reference for this exact split and label
type \cite{tripclick-strong-baselines}. Selecting entirely on
validation, we found two config choices that close this gap: Porter
stemming (val nDCG@10 $0.235 \to 0.270$) and weighting the title field
ten times body weight in a title/body-split FTS5 index (val nDCG@10
climbing to a clear plateau at that ratio, $0.327$, MRR peaking there
and declining beyond it). A single, one-time sanity check of the frozen
config against withheld test --- not a selection criterion --- gives
nDCG@10 $0.315$, \emph{above} the published reference. We report this
because an under-tuned baseline mechanically inflates the apparent
value of anything compared against it: every downstream conclusion in
this paper changed once this fix was in place, a precondition for the
rest of the study rather than an implementation footnote.

\subsection{Implementation}

Sparse retrieval uses BM25 over a SQLite FTS5 index \cite{sqlite-fts5}
rather than a JVM Lucene index, forced by a JVM crash under Pyserini
\cite{pyserini} on our hardware; two further silent-corruption risks
surfaced at scale, the same category as Section~\ref{sec:bm25-fix}'s
baseline gap and Section~\ref{sec:defect}'s telemetry defect: a
conjunctive default MATCH operator (collapsing recall without an
error) and an unindexed companion lookup that degrades to a full table
scan ($18\times$ slower at $1.5$M rows). Cross-encoder
reranking (\texttt{cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2}
\cite{minilm-cross-encoder}) applies to the top 100 BM25 candidates,
reranking the top 50. Generation uses a locally hosted, 4-bit quantized
2--3B-parameter model, capped at two calls per query episode with a
32--64 token structured output, so the language model is never the
entire decision surface, only one typed tool within it. Code, configs,
and analysis scripts are available at an anonymized repository for
review: \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/agentic-query-expansion-v2/}.

\subsection{Verifier Calibration Methodology}
\label{sec:calib-method}

Following Section~\ref{sec:protocol}, we collect calibration episodes
(one expansion action per query, true nDCG@10 change against click
labels) on TAIL \emph{validation} ($n=1147$--$1168$). The calibration
action comes from the same \texttt{ActionSelector} policy used
everywhere else (Section~\ref{sec:controller}), not separate sampling,
so its operator distribution matches the agent's actual usage by
construction. Operator identity is not itself a verifier feature
(Section~\ref{sec:observability}): the fitted verifier estimates
generic post-action utility, marginalized over whichever operator was
selected, not utility conditional on a specific operator. We compare
three feature families of increasing size (six retrieval-telemetry
deltas; plus four reranker/length features; plus three dense-embedding
features) against three model classes each (fixed-weight, 5-fold CV
linear, 5-fold CV GBDT), under Pearson correlation (Table~\ref{tab:rq1}),
to characterize the detection ceiling rather than accept the first
plausible fit --- never itself reported as evidence the signal
generalizes (Section~\ref{sec:rq1} does that separately, from one
frozen touch of test). Recovery thresholds are calibrated the same
way, from candidate cutoffs on validation.

\subsection{Metrics}

Retrieval quality is nDCG@10, Recall@100, and MRR, plus the fraction of
queries helped/unchanged/harmed relative to a baseline. For the
recovery study (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}) we report Detection Recall
$P(\text{intervened}\mid\text{harmful})$, False Alarm Rate
$P(\text{intervened}\mid\text{healthy})$, and recovery gain in mean
nDCG@10 with vs.\ without recovery on identically corrupted episodes.
We additionally report a retrospective \emph{oracle ceiling}: per
query, $\max(\text{baseline}, \text{treatment})$ under true click
labels, diagnosing whether headroom exists independent of whether our
qrel-free verifier can find it (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}). Significance is
via paired bootstrap (10{,}000 resamples) throughout.

\subsection{Detection Is Not Actionability}
\label{sec:detect-vs-act}

That action-utility model answers a hard question: given telemetry
before \emph{and} after a specific action, did it help? A logically
prior, cheaper question uses only pre-action telemetry: is unexpanded
retrieval already unhealthy? We build a second model from the same
eight retrieval-telemetry features, labeling a query ``poor'' when
BM25 surfaces zero relevant documents in the top 10 (test prevalence
$0.450$), selected by 5-fold CV on validation and touched once on
frozen test, identically to Section~\ref{sec:calib-method}'s protocol
--- Table~\ref{tab:rq1-test}'s last row.

On both val and test, telemetry moderately predictive of
\emph{whether} retrieval is failing
($\text{AUC}=0.649$, 95\% CI $[0.618,0.680]$) is markedly weaker at
predicting whether a \emph{specific candidate action fixes it}
($\text{AUC}=0.552$, 95\% CI $[0.518,0.586]$) --- both frozen
single-touch test numbers from the same features and protocol, not the
same result at different noise levels. Non-overlapping CIs alone don't
formally test the difference; a paired bootstrap on the $n{=}1157$
acted-upon queries (failure AUC on this subset: $0.643$) gives
$+0.0905$, 95\% CI $[+0.0446,+0.1361]$, excluding zero
(\texttt{scripts/28}) --- the gap itself is statistically established,
not just the two point estimates. \textbf{RQ1's answer}: qrel-free
telemetry is moderately predictive of whether something is wrong, but
even after observing the retrieval consequences of its own action, it
is only weakly informative about whether that action actually helped.
Both RQ2's control result and RQ3's fault-specific pattern trace back
to this weakness.

\section{RQ3: Which Failures Are Recoverable?}
\label{sec:rq3}

RQ1 and RQ2 leave open whether observability has \emph{any} practical
value, or whether a weak general detector is useless. We test a
narrower claim: that the same weak verifier can serve as a safety net
against \emph{large, obvious} failures even though it cannot reliably
judge \emph{subtle, natural} ones. We inject three fault types at
their natural point of corruption --- an unsupported entity appended
to the expansion, a grounding passage swapped for a non-supporting
one, and the reranker disabled --- and compare, on identical corrupted
episodes, an always-accept ablation against the real controller.

\subsection{A Defect That Changed the Answer}
\label{sec:defect}

An early pass found the reranker-disabled fault essentially
undetectable (recall $0.185$) while the other two looked strong.
Tracing this revealed a real defect: the reranked ranking was
computed, at real latency cost, but never actually applied to the
accepted ranking --- disabling the reranker was mechanically a no-op.
Fixing it exposed a second, entangled defect: the verifier's telemetry
was still computed from the pre-rerank ranking. We corrected both and
re-ran calibration to confirm the deployed verifier did not need
retraining (corrected Pearson $r=0.146$ vs.\ $0.134$). We report this
because a component computed purely for observability is
architecturally easy to leave disconnected from the outcome it
explains, and the error looked coherent --- unrealistically strong ---
until an implausible near-zero effect prompted investigation.

The three fault types (Table~\ref{tab:rq3}) split into three distinct
outcomes: no single answer to ``is recovery useful,'' only a
per-failure-type one. \textbf{Entity injection} is detected almost
every time but flagged almost as often, netting out non-significant.
\textbf{Grounding corruption} is the one clean recovery success.
\textbf{Reranker disabled} is never detected: the controller
essentially always accepts.

\textbf{How, mechanistically.} Fitted weights alone show which
features the verifier ignores, not what a fault \emph{does} to its
score: $\Delta x_j = \mathbb{E}[x_j^{\text{fault}} - x_j^{\text{clean}}]$,
$\text{Contribution}_j = w_j \cdot \Delta x_j$ ($n{=}79$/fault) --- a
decomposition against the verifier's fitted (co-varying) features, not
an independent causal effect of feature $j$ alone. The clearest case
is reranker disabled: its dominant contribution is large and
\emph{positive}, from \texttt{disagreement} moving in the direction
the verifier reads as improvement --- it does not merely evade weak
reranker-derived features, it makes the corrupted state score higher,
so the controller is actively misled, not merely blind.

\textbf{RQ3's answer is that recoverability is fault-specific}: a real
net gain needs a score shift small enough for per-episode
discrimination (grounding), is a wash when the shift is large and
uniform (entity injection), and a fault is disguised as an improvement
when it moves a strongly-weighted feature the \emph{wrong} way
(reranker) --- a verifier can be actively adversarial to a fault it
was never calibrated against.

\subsection{A Diagnostic Rule, Tested on Three Held-Out Faults}
\label{sec:diagnostic}

The three faults' net contributions ($\sum_j w_j\Delta x_j$,
Table~\ref{tab:diagnostic}) and recall/false-alarm pairs move
together: strongly negative pushes the score down regardless of true
harm, giving high recall but comparably high false alarms (entity
injection); strongly positive does the opposite (reranker disabled);
only near-zero leaves room for per-episode variation to separate
harmful from healthy cases --- the one fault with a significant net
gain (grounding). This needs only paired telemetry, no relevance
labels: it is usable after a telemetry-only probe of a new fault,
without labels or a full recovery-quality evaluation.

\section{Does the Finding Replicate on a Second Corpus?}
\label{sec:nq}

To check whether Section~\ref{sec:detect-vs-act}'s finding is specific
to TripClick, we replicate the detection-vs-actionability comparison on
BEIR Natural Questions~\cite{beir,natural-questions}, an open-domain
corpus unrelated to TripClick's clinical queries. NQ ships as one
3{,}452-query split with no train/val/test partition, so we carve a
disjoint, fixed-seed ($42$) val/test split (800/600), freeze a fresh
BM25 config and detectors on val only (same features/model classes as
TripClick's, refit since corpus-specific), then evaluate on test. The
initial $n{=}600$ touch was a valid, frozen single touch whose
action-utility AUC CI straddled chance --- too imprecise to resolve the
ordering. Before evaluating any of the remaining $2{,}052$ NQ queries,
we fixed an all-or-nothing extension over that entire remainder: no
early stopping, no retuning, pooled result reported regardless of
outcome ($n{=}2{,}652$).
Table~\ref{tab:nq} reports the pooled
result: as on TripClick, Static QE does not significantly beat BM25
despite oracle-capturable headroom ($p<0.001$), and the same
ordering replicates, now decisively --- pre-action telemetry is more
predictive of retrieval failure (AUC $0.670$) than post-action
telemetry is of intervention utility (AUC $0.550$, 95\% CI
$[0.515,0.585]$, clearly above chance). Failure-detection AUC uses all
$2{,}652$ queries; action-utility AUC and its paired comparison use only
the $2{,}599$ on which an expansion action was actually executed (the
rest were NO\_EXPAND). That paired bootstrap of the AUC difference
excludes zero ($+0.115$, 95\% CI $[+0.075,+0.154]$,
\texttt{scripts/26}). This is an
independent second-corpus replication of
Section~\ref{sec:detect-vs-act}'s ordering, not evidence the finding
generalizes broadly across domains --- one further corpus, $2{,}652$
held-out queries.

\section{Discussion}

RQ1--RQ3, the oracle analysis, the diagnostic rule, and the cross-split
check sketch one finding. Section~\ref{sec:detect-vs-act} is
load-bearing: telemetry is moderately predictive of whether retrieval
is failing (AUC $0.649$) but weakly informative about whether a
specific action on an ordinary query will fix it (AUC $0.552$). RQ2
shows the consequence: a closed loop built on the weaker signal adds
nothing over a matched static pipeline, and the oracle/selectivity-frontier
analyses show $+0.0246$ nDCG@10 of headroom that the verifier reaches
at most $12\%$ of. RQ3's attribution shows this is a per-fault property,
sharp enough in one case that the verifier reads the fault as an
improvement. The diagnostic rule (Section~\ref{sec:diagnostic}) provides
the clearest positive evidence: for \emph{large, structured} faults,
the same weights characterize which regime a new fault falls into after
a telemetry-only probe.
Section~\ref{sec:robustness} bounds this differently, holding the
fault fixed and varying query frequency instead: the diagnostic rule
transfers to held-out fault types after a telemetry-only probe,
whereas the action-utility ranking does not transfer across
query-frequency regimes.

The BM25 baseline and reranker-wiring defects both looked coherent
rather than broken, so neither surfaced from an error message. A
frozen protocol with a mandatory sanity check caught the first; the
second surfaced only from distrusting a result that seemed too good.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The oracle ceiling (Section~\ref{sec:oracle}) is retrospective, using
test-set click labels directly --- a valid upper bound, never
deployable. The generator is a locally hosted, quantized 2--3B
parameter model under a two-call budget; a larger generator might
change RQ1's ceiling or RQ2's null result. Cross-split robustness
covers TripClick's three buckets, and the core detection-vs-actionability
finding replicates on one further corpus (Section~\ref{sec:nq}); broader
coverage remains untested. Click-derived qrels are an imperfect
relevance proxy inherited from TripClick. The diagnostic rule
(Section~\ref{sec:diagnostic}) orders all six fault types' detection
recall correctly, including three held out, even though our own
guesses about each fault's regime were often wrong; six fault types is
still a small, hand-designed set. It is also post-probe, not
zero-shot: it needs a fault's own telemetry shift to compute. The
failure-detection model uses one binary
definition of ``poor retrieval''; a graded definition could shift its
measured AUC.

\section{Conclusion}

We evaluated a closed-loop, observability-guided query-expansion agent
under a corrected protocol separating model selection from final
evaluation --- surfacing the paper's first finding: a
validation-selected BM25 correction materially changed the downstream
comparisons. Runtime observability is moderately predictive of
retrieval failure (detection AUC $0.649$), but autonomous recovery
needs a stronger property the same telemetry does not deliver:
predicting whether a specific intervention will help (action-utility
AUC $0.552$, below chance outside its calibration split). That gap is
why the closed-loop agent does not outperform static expansion and
neither outperforms BM25, despite $+0.0246$ nDCG@10 of headroom going
uncaptured. The
recovery study shows this is a per-fault property with distinct,
interpretable telemetry signatures --- in one case the verifier is actively misled, not merely
blind --- and, tested on three held-out faults, supports label-free
characterization of a new failure's detection regime after a
telemetry-only probe; converting that into a significant gain still needs a full
evaluation. The same ranking inverts across query-frequency regimes,
yet the core detection-vs-actionability gap itself replicates on a
second, unrelated corpus (BEIR NQ, Section~\ref{sec:nq}). Both
implementation defects reported here (Sections~\ref{sec:bm25-fix},
\ref{sec:defect}) are methodological findings in their own right, not
incidental fixes.
