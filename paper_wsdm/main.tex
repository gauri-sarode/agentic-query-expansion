\title{Query Performance Prediction Is Not Benefit Prediction: Observability for Selective Retrieval Interventions}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}
\affiliation{\institution{Affiliation withheld for double-blind review}}

\begin{abstract}
Selective retrieval interventions, such as LLM query expansion (QE),
cross-encoder reranking, and pseudo-relevance feedback (PRF), are
often gated on a query performance predictor: intervene when retrieval
looks like it is failing. We show why failure is the wrong target and
measure by how much. Writing the decision as maximizing an
intervention's expected gain given observable telemetry, we decompose
an oracle selector's gain exactly into gain on failing queries, the
most any policy acting only on them can recover at zero cost, and gain
on healthy queries, which it never sees. On TripClick and BEIR NQ,
across eight intervention, corpus, and retriever settings, this
failure-only ceiling is $8$--$57\%$ of the oracle's gain; a
pre-registered run on an untouched benchmark (ArguAna) gives
$18$--$39\%$. Detection is ordinary QPP (AUC $0.62$--$0.70$), and its
lead over harm prediction vanishes on TripClick and NQ queries the
intervention changes. At matched budgets, failure, NQC, and pre-action benefit
routing, up to gradient boosting and query embeddings, stay near
random, and failure routing spends most of its budget on failing
queries the intervention leaves unchanged. In an exploratory analysis,
observing part of the intervention helps where it usually helps: on
NQ, routing reranking on cross-encoder scores of the first ten
candidates beats failure routing by $+0.024$ nDCG@10 and free NQC
routing by $+0.020$ at equal measured cost, but not on TripClick or
ArguAna.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Retrieval interventions help some queries and harm others. LLM query
expansion (QE) can rewrite a query using the evidence it
retrieves~\cite{csqe}, yet it harms a measurable fraction of
queries~\cite{weller-qe-fails,qe-fails-unfamiliar}; cross-encoder
reranking and pseudo-relevance feedback (PRF) are equally uneven. The
natural response is to intervene selectively, and the natural gate is
a query performance predictor (QPP): intervene when the current
ranking looks like it is failing~\cite{amati-selective,adaptive-rag}.
That the gate's target differs from the quantity a policy needs is
not new: selective-QE work predicts expansion benefit
directly~\cite{selective-qe}, and uplift modelling shows that
targeting high-risk cases is not targeting the cases a treatment
helps~\cite{ascarza-futility,causal-classification}. What is missing
is how much a failure gate gives up, why, and whether the telemetry a
deployed system can observe, its \emph{observability}, can do better.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\textbf{(C1)} A decision framing of selective intervention under
observable telemetry, with an exact decomposition of the gain no
policy acting only on failing queries can capture, at zero and
positive cost (Section~\ref{sec:framework}).
\textbf{(C2)} Its measurement for LLM QE, reranking, and PRF on three
corpora, one pre-registered and untouched: the \emph{failure-only
ceiling} is $8$--$57\%$ of the oracle's gain, the pre-action
predictors we test do not recover the rest, and diagnostics show why.
\textbf{(C3)} Routing under observability and measured cost, from
pre-action telemetry through partial probes of the intervention to
post-action verification.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{QPP and selective QE.} Pre- and post-retrieval QPP
(Clarity~\cite{qpp-clarity}, WIG~\cite{wig}, NQC~\cite{nqc}) predicts
query difficulty. Cronen-Townsend et al.~\cite{selective-qe} decide
whether to expand by comparing the clarity of expanded and unexpanded
rankings; Amati et al.~\cite{amati-selective} gate expansion on
difficulty predictors; Lv and Zhai~\cite{lv-zhai-adaptive} predict a
per-query feedback weight; Collins-Thompson~\cite{collins-thompson-risk}
trades expansion reward against risk. These works evaluate the gated
system in aggregate; they neither bound what a failure gate can
recover nor compare gates at matched budgets.

\textbf{LLM QE and adaptive retrieval.} Query2doc~\cite{query2doc},
MuGI~\cite{mugi}, and CSQE~\cite{csqe} expand unconditionally;
generative expansions often hurt strong retrievers~\cite{weller-qe-fails}
and fail on unfamiliar or ambiguous queries~\cite{qe-fails-unfamiliar}.
Kotte~\cite{not-all-queries-need-rewriting} gates prompt-only rewriting
for dense retrievers without reliable gain. Sarode~\cite{sarode-cods}
finds that a learned selector over candidate LLM rewrites captures little
oracle headroom and transfers inconsistently; we ask whether to intervene
at all, across three interventions, and bound failure-restricted policies.
Adaptive-RAG~\cite{adaptive-rag} routes on question complexity and
Mallen et al.~\cite{mallen-adaptive} retrieve only for less popular
entities; cascade rankers~\cite{cascade-ranking} spend costly stages
only on promising candidates, and cutoff and efficiency
predictors~\cite{culpepper-cutoff,tonellotto-selective} set per-query
reranking depth or pruning; QPP itself is weaker for neural
rankers~\cite{faggioli-qpp-neural}. None separates a gate's detection accuracy from its benefit
prediction. In uplift terms~\cite{causal-classification}, our setting
is unusually clean: both outcomes are observed for every query
offline, so the uplift label is exact up to relevance-label noise.

\section{Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

For a query $q$, let $R_0$ be the original ranking and $R_a$ the
ranking after intervention $a$, with metric $M$ (nDCG@10). The
intervention's effect is $\Delta_a(q)=M(R_a,q)-M(R_0,q)$ and its cost
$c_a$ ($\Delta_0=c_0=0$). With full information the best decision is
$a^*(q)=\arg\max_a\{\Delta_a(q)-\kappa c_a\}$, for a cost weight
$\kappa$. A deployed controller sees only telemetry $Z$, so the best
it can do is $\pi^*(Z)=\arg\max_a\{\mathbb{E}[\Delta_a\mid Z]-\kappa c_a\}$.
What matters is therefore what $Z$ reveals about $\Delta_a$, not about
$M(R_0)$. Two information sets differ in cost: \emph{pre-action}
telemetry $Z_0$ describes $R_0$ and costs no intervention, though
computing it is not free; \emph{post-action}
telemetry $Z_1$ also describes $R_a$ and exists only after $c_a$ is
paid. A \emph{probe} observes part of the intervention, such as
cross-encoder scores of a few candidates, at part of its cost
(Section~\ref{sec:probe}). A failure gate replaces $\mathbb{E}[\Delta_a\mid Z_0]$ with
$P(F\mid Z_0)$, where $F=\mathbf{1}[M(R_0,q)=0]$.

\textbf{The failure-only ceiling.} Fix one intervention with $c_a=1$,
so $\kappa$ is its cost per query in nDCG@10 units. The oracle selector earns
$G_{\text{orc}}(\kappa)=\mathbb{E}[(\Delta-\kappa)_+]$, and a
\emph{perfect} failure gate, which intervenes iff $F=1$, earns
$G_{F}(\kappa)=\mathbb{E}[(\Delta-\kappa)F]$. Because $M\ge 0$, a
failing query cannot be harmed ($\Delta\ge 0$ when $F=1$), and
\[
G_{\text{orc}}(\kappa)-G_{F}(\kappa)
=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[(\Delta-\kappa)_+(1-F)]}_{\text{gain on healthy queries}}
+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[(\kappa-\Delta)_+F]}_{\text{uncovered cost on failures}}.
\]
At $\kappa=0$ the second term vanishes and the gate's share of the
oracle is $\mathbb{E}[\Delta_+F]/\mathbb{E}[\Delta_+]$. The identity is
elementary; its use is that both terms are measurable offline for any
intervention. For $\kappa>0$ it is the exact regret of the policy that
intervenes on every failing query (the second term includes failing
queries helped by less than $\kappa$), not a ceiling: a detector that
also skips such queries can beat that policy. At $\kappa=0$ it bounds
any policy that intervenes only on failing queries; it does not bound
a policy built from a failure \emph{score} that also acts on healthy
queries.

\section{Setup}

\textbf{Data.} TripClick~\cite{tripclick}: 1.52M health documents, the
TAIL bucket's validation and test splits (1{,}175 queries each), with
click-derived relevance labels. BEIR NQ~\cite{beir,natural-questions}:
2.68M passages, human labels; 800 validation and a disjoint
2{,}052-query test set. BEIR ArguAna~\cite{arguana}: 8{,}674
arguments, 1{,}406 queries whose relevant document is the
counterargument (Section~\ref{sec:arguana}).

\textbf{Interventions.} \emph{LLM QE}: BM25 over SQLite FTS5; a
deterministic rule picks one of four expansion operators or none, and \texttt{qwen2.5:3b} (4-bit,
greedy, $\le2$ calls) writes a corpus-grounded expansion. On TripClick
and under dense retrieval (\texttt{bge-small-en-v1.5}~\cite{bge-embedding})
the expanded ranking's top candidates are reranked by a MiniLM
cross-encoder~\cite{minilm-cross-encoder}; the original is not, so
those labels include the reranker's effect. TripClick's deployed QE
fuses original and expanded rankings (RRF); NQ's uses the expanded
ranking. \emph{Reranking}: the cross-encoder scores the BM25 top 100;
its top 50 lead. \emph{PRF}: RM3-style~\cite{relevance-models}
weights from the top $d$ documents select $t$ terms whose BM25 ranking
is interpolated with the original (weight $\lambda$); $(d,t,\lambda)$
is tuned on validation over 108 settings.

\textbf{Labels and models.} \emph{Failure}: $F=1$ ($45\%$ of TripClick
and $49\%$ of NQ test queries under BM25). \emph{Harmed}: $\Delta<0$,
ties not harmed. Detection is a logistic regression on 8 pre-action
features (score shape, term coverage, rare-term IDF, entity overlap,
coherence, perturbation stability; 6 under dense). Action utility is
fit on $\Delta$ from post-action features (changes in score shape,
coverage, and coherence, ranking overlap, rewrite drift). For
pre-action routing, a \emph{benefit} model regresses $\Delta$ on the 8
pre-action features alone. Every model and threshold is fit on
validation and scored once on test; intervals are paired bootstraps
over queries ($10^4$ resamples).

\textbf{Evidence ledger.} Only the ArguAna results
(Section~\ref{sec:arguana}) are independent confirmation: every NQ
query and the TripClick TAIL test set have been inspected.
\emph{Fixed before a test run}: the NQ, reranking, and PRF runs.
\emph{Pre-committed analyses of inspected traces}: the AUCs, ceiling,
budget routing, and Holm correction. \emph{Exploratory}: TripClick test was used during
development, the changed-query columns of
Table~\ref{tab:auc} were added after seeing the main results, and the
NQ B1/B2 runs, diagnostics, and probes of Section~\ref{sec:probe}
followed protocols frozen before computing but after the test sets
were inspected.
Pre-commitments and scripts are in the anonymized repository:
\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/agentic-query-expansion-8939/}.

A perfect failure detector would capture $8$--$57\%$ of the oracle's
gain (Table~\ref{tab:ceiling}; $18$--$39\%$ on ArguAna), though it
never makes a query worse. The share follows how often an
intervention rescues a failing query relative to how often it helps a
healthy one: reranking rescues often ($17\%$ and $46\%$ of failing
queries), so half its headroom lies there; LLM QE rescues
$6$--$15\%$ (a third to two-fifths); PRF on TripClick almost never
($0.8\%$). The rest sits in healthy queries that the same intervention
also harms often ($19$--$56\%$), which only a benefit prediction can
separate.

Costs widen the gap. At $\kappa=0.02$, the uncovered cost on failures
reaches $0.004$--$0.011$, and intervening on every failing query
loses for PRF on both corpora and dense TripClick QE, while the
oracle still gains $0.011$--$0.027$. At $\kappa>0$ this is the regret
of that one policy, not a ceiling: skipping failing queries the
intervention would not help enough could do better, but needs
information beyond the binary failure label.

\textbf{Which queries a failure gate picks.} At a $25\%$ budget
(exploratory; prespecified effect categories), $45$--$68\%$ of the
queries failure routing selects on TripClick and NQ ($30$--$36\%$ on
ArguAna) are failing queries the intervention leaves unchanged, and
rescues appear among them at about their base rate. Healthy queries
gaining at least $0.10$ hold $42$--$83\%$ of the oracle's gain and
small gains at most $9\%$ (except ArguAna PRF), so what is missed is
large gains, not noise around zero. Among the queries each pre-action
scorer selects (failure, NQC, linear or boosted benefit), the share
helped is within $-0.11$ to $+0.08$ of its base rate, and they capture
$13$--$41\%$ of the gain of the oracle's selection, against
$25$--$35\%$ for random.

\textbf{Detection is ordinary QPP.} Detection AUC is $0.62$--$0.70$;
NQC~\cite{nqc} matches it on TripClick ($0.647$ vs.\ $0.649$) and
beats it on NQ ($0.721$ vs.\ $0.680$), and it stays significant within

\textbf{Its lead over harm prediction is structural.} Detection beats
harm AUC in seven of eight settings after Holm correction
(Table~\ref{tab:auc}), and in six of eight when harm requires
$\Delta<-0.05$ or $-0.10$. But failing queries cannot be harmed and
are mostly left unchanged, so the harm label is largely a failure
indicator. On queries the intervention changed, detection drops to
$0.47$--$0.58$ and every TripClick and NQ BM25 gap vanishes; only
dense NQ keeps one, and ArguAna PRF's rests on $38$ queries ($21$

TripClick QE against the reranked original ($+0.059$

\textbf{Pre-action routing.} At every budget, each scorer that
decides before intervening stays close to random routing and far
below the oracle (Table~\ref{tab:routing}). Failure routing beats
random by at most $0.006$ at any budget, and falls below it at $75\%$
on TripClick for QE and reranking, up to $50\%$ for PRF on NQ, and at
every budget for reranking on ArguAna (Figure~\ref{fig:probe}). Averaged over budgets, NQC is
no better than the detector, and benefit routing beats failure
routing only for reranking on NQ ($+0.009$ $[+0.003,+0.015]$), where
it leads at every budget; averaging over $10$--$75\%$ only
(exploratory; all policies coincide at $100\%$) leaves the same single
Holm survivor ($+0.011$ $[+0.004,+0.019]$). Per budget
(exploratory, uncorrected) it is $+0.010$, $+0.009$, $+0.016$, and
$+0.009$ at $10$--$75\%$, its interval excluding zero except at
$25\%$; ArguAna's B1 reranking lead is $+0.018$ $[+0.001,+0.033]$ at
$10\%$, its intervals spanning zero at $25$--$75\%$. The benefit model is
linear, but ArguAna's B1 and B2 (Section~\ref{sec:arguana}), fit on NQ
validation (exploratory), do no better: neither beats failure routing
after Holm for any intervention, and the best recovers $8\%$ of the
oracle-minus-failure headroom. This is an empirical property of these
features and models, not a bound. For LLM QE, the deployed
failure-selective policy halves generator calls and on NQ recovers
most of unconditional QE's loss ($+0.015$ $[+0.008,+0.022]$).

To test the conclusions on data never inspected for this work, we
pre-registered a run on BEIR ArguAna, a counterargument-retrieval task,
before downloading it: hypotheses, a debate-grouped split
($701/275/430$), the same three interventions (LLM QE as on NQ, without
a reranker), three new pre-action benefit predictors, and
Holm-corrected primary contrasts. Each query is itself a corpus
document, and $108$ arguments are cross-listed under two ids; every
search drops the query's own and text-identical documents, and
duplicates share a split. These fixes, and three primary contrasts
adopted from an externally written protocol, were dated amendments
made before any label was read
(\texttt{configs/\allowbreak arguana\_\allowbreak precommit.json}). Predictors are fit on
training queries, selected on validation, and scored once on test.
B1, B3, and the failure router use 10 pre-action features (the 8
above, NQC, query length). B1 is LightGBM regression on $\Delta$ (two
depth/leaves/L2 settings, chosen by validation MSE). B2 uses the
embedding alone: the inverse-distance-weighted mean $\Delta$ of the
$k$ nearest training queries under frozen \texttt{bge-small-en-v1.5}
embeddings ($k$ by validation MSE). B3 is LightGBM quantile
regression; its registered quantile choice by pinball loss is unsound
(losses at different quantiles are incomparable), so a secondary B3
fixes it at $0.25$. Grids: \texttt{scripts/54\_baseline\_runner.py}.

On ArguAna every intervention loses on average (reranking $-0.071$
$[-0.108,-0.034]$, LLM QE $-0.163$), yet the oracle could still gain
$0.053$ and $0.010$ budget-averaged, so the question is purely one of
selection. PRF barely acts here (nDCG@10 changes for $9\%$ of queries), and its
registered setting scored slightly below BM25 on validation ($0.4899$
vs.\ $0.4903$), so its rows are uninformative. Pre-action routing again stays near random
(Table~\ref{tab:arguana}). For reranking, B1 minus failure routing is
$+0.015$ $[-0.001,+0.028]$ (Holm $p=0.13$), B2 minus failure $+0.008$
$[-0.005,+0.020]$ ($p=0.37$), and B3 versus B1 under a budget cap
$+0.006$ $[-0.011,+0.023]$ ($p=0.52$); the best recovers $16\%$ of the
oracle-minus-failure headroom, below the pre-registered $25\%$ bar.
Excluding $100\%$ (exploratory), B1 minus failure is
$+0.019$ $[-0.001,+0.035]$. Per budget, failure routing of reranking is
below random at every budget and B1 above it
(Figure~\ref{fig:probe}); at $10\%$, $42\%$ of the queries failure
routing picks are harmed. A
secondary pre-registered family (refit on train plus validation, wider
grids) agrees: none of nine contrasts survives Holm. ArguAna is thus a hard, out-of-domain selection
problem, not proof that pre-action prediction cannot work; B1's
$+0.015$ may be real but below what $430$ queries resolve. Costs
differ widely: PRF takes $3$\,ms per query, LLM QE $6.4$\,s, and the 8
pre-action features $0.32$\,s.

A probe sits between pre- and post-action telemetry. In an exploratory
analysis (protocol frozen before any probe score was computed, on test
sets already inspected), we score only the first $k\in\{5,10,20\}$
BM25 candidates with the cross-encoder, regress the $\Delta$ of full
reranking on nine probe features (score maximum, mean, and spread;
the first candidate's score and its gap to the maximum; the argmax
position; rank correlation with BM25; NQC; query length) with B1's
model and selection rule, and fully rerank the top-scoring queries,
reusing the probe's scores. Each policy is charged its median measured
latency (50 validation queries per corpus): full reranking takes
$0.48$, $1.11$, and $1.26$\,s on NQ, TripClick, and ArguAna, a
ten-candidate probe $0.05$--$0.13$\,s, and pre-action telemetry
$1.58$, $0.35$, and $0.32$\,s.

On NQ, where reranking usually helps (Figure~\ref{fig:probe}), Probe-10 routing beats failure routing by
$+0.024$ $[+0.017,+0.031]$ and B1 by $+0.017$ $[+0.011,+0.022]$ over
$10$--$75\%$ reranked (both Holm-significant over six tests), and its
predictions rank $\Delta$ better (Spearman $0.25$ $[0.21,0.29]$,
against $0.02$ for the failure score and $0.09$ for B1). Failure and
B1 routing pay for NQ's pre-action telemetry, whose perturbation search
spans $2.68$M passages and takes longer than reranking every query, so
always reranking dominates them; against free NQC (post-review,
exploratory), probe routing gains $+0.030$ $[+0.022,+0.037]$ at matched
fraction and $+0.020$ $[+0.012,+0.028]$ at equal measured cost. On
TripClick and ArguAna, where reranking loses on average, probes do not
help: no contrast with failure, B1, or NQC routing is significant at
matched fraction (failure and B1: TripClick $+0.002$ and $-0.002$,
ArguAna $+0.009$ and $-0.010$), and B1 ranks $\Delta$ at least as well
(Spearman $0.08$ and $0.10$ vs.\ $0.05$; differences span zero).
A $20$-candidate probe gains $+0.032$ over failure on NQ.

Post-action verification, the full-cost end, cuts TripClick's
reranking loss from $-0.028$ to $-0.001$ $[-0.005,+0.003]$ (the
thresholded failure gate: $-0.010$), keeps $94\%$ of NQ's reranked
results ($+0.223$ vs.\ $+0.225$), and gains $+0.004$
$[-0.005,+0.015]$ on ArguAna.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

Failure prediction is the usual gate, and it targets the wrong
quantity. Two kinds of result support this. One is proven: at zero
cost, a policy that intervenes only on failing queries is capped by
how often the intervention rescues them, a cap that differs nearly
sevenfold across interventions. The other is empirical: the
pre-action predictors we test, up to gradient boosting and query
embeddings, pick helped queries at about their base rate, and
failure routing spends most of its budget where the intervention does
nothing. The second alone would fit `no pre-action signal we test
predicts benefit'; the first is what makes failure specifically the
wrong target. Pre-action telemetry describes the original ranking,
while $\Delta_a$ depends on what the intervention retrieves. The
probes support this only in part: a little of the intervention's own
output locates its gains where it usually helps (NQ), not where it
usually harms.

\textbf{Limitations.} One small (3B) generator; reranking and PRF only
under BM25, with one cross-encoder and one PRF family. Three corpora,
one of them ($430$ test queries) untouched, which leaves the ArguAna
contrasts underpowered for differences below about $0.015$;
under dense retrieval, retriever strength and domain are confounded,
and the dense QE labels include the reranker. TripClick's labels are
click-derived and sparse. The cost weight $\kappa$ is illustrative;
latencies are single-machine medians, and probes cover reranking
only. The TripClick QE benefit model is fit to the unfused
validation label, since no fused validation ranking exists. Apart from
ArguAna, all analyses reuse inspected test sets; every pre-committed
quantity is reported in full in the repository.

\section{Conclusion}

Failure detection is ordinary QPP; at zero cost it caps a controller
acting only on failing queries at $8$--$57\%$ of an oracle's gain. The
pre-action predictors we test pick helped queries at about their base
rate; observing
part of the intervention helps where it usually helps, at a fraction
of the cost, but not where it usually harms. Selective-intervention
work should report the failure-only ceiling and cost-aware routing
curves, not only AUC.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

All data (TripClick, BEIR NQ, and BEIR ArguAna) are existing
research benchmarks used under their licenses; TripClick is used for
non-commercial research, and no model or per-query output fit on it is
released. TripClick's click logs are aggregate, de-identified
interactions, and we make no attempt to re-identify searchers. No
human-subjects data was collected. The generator is a small, locally
hosted model used only to rewrite queries within a bounded action
space. Our central finding cautions against deploying generative
interventions on the strength of a failure detector alone.
