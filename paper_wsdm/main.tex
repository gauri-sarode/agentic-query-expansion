\title{Observability-Guided LLM Query Expansion in Search: The Actionability Gap}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}
\affiliation{\institution{Affiliation withheld for double-blind review}}

\begin{abstract}
LLM query expansion (QE) helps some queries and harms others, which
motivates applying it selectively. A selective controller needs two
predictions: whether retrieval is failing (\emph{detection}) and
whether a specific expansion will help or harm it (\emph{action
utility}). We
measure both on the same queries from qrel-free retrieval telemetry,
on TripClick and BEIR Natural Questions, under BM25 and a dense
retriever. On the queries the system expands, detection AUC is
$0.62$--$0.70$, and under BM25 no better than a standard unsupervised
predictor (NQC), while
predicting whether the expansion harms retrieval reaches only
$0.55$--$0.61$: a paired-significant gap in three of four settings. The
gap is largely structural. Expansion leaves $84$--$94\%$ of failing
queries still failing, and among queries whose nDCG the expansion
changed, the gap persists only on dense NQ. The policy picture
matches. A perfect failure detector would recover only $42\%$
(TripClick) and $33\%$ (NQ) of an oracle selector's gain; the rest lies
in healthy queries that expansion sometimes helps and sometimes harms.
Our failure-selective controller halves generator calls, and on NQ it
significantly reduces the harm of unconditional expansion, but no
policy measurably beats BM25 alone.
\end{abstract}

\maketitle

\section{Introduction}

LLMs can rewrite a query into pseudo-documents, paraphrases, or
disambiguating context, and conditioning the rewrite on retrieved
evidence improves on query-only rewriting~\cite{csqe}. Applied
unconditionally, though, LLM QE harms a measurable fraction of
queries~\cite{weller-qe-fails,qe-fails-unfamiliar}. Selective
expansion is the natural response, and it needs two predictions:

\[
\underbrace{P(\text{failure} \mid x)}_{\text{detection}}
\qquad\text{vs.}\qquad
\underbrace{P(\Delta\text{nDCG@10} < 0 \mid x, a)}_{\text{action utility (harm)}}
\]

Detection asks whether the current ranking is already bad. Action
utility asks whether a \emph{specific} expansion $a$ will make it
worse; its mirror, whether $a$ will help, matters as much for a
policy (Section~\ref{sec:worth}). That the two differ is not new: selective-QE work predicts
expansion benefit directly~\cite{selective-qe,amati-selective}, and
uplift modelling shows that targeting high-risk cases is not the same
as targeting cases a treatment helps~\cite{ascarza-futility,causal-classification}.
What is missing for LLM QE is a measurement of both predictions on
the same queries, and of what each one is worth to a policy. We use
\emph{observability} for qrel-free telemetry a search system records
about its own behaviour (score distributions, coverage, ranking
stability, reranker agreement), the signal a deployed controller can
actually see.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\textbf{(C1)} A paired, same-query measurement of detection and
action-utility AUC on two corpora under BM25 and dense retrieval, with
the label's tie rule, a standard QPP baseline, and a combined
pre+post-action model reported alongside.
\textbf{(C2)} A decomposition of selective-QE headroom: how much of an
oracle selector's gain perfect failure detection alone would recover,
and why the remainder needs action-utility prediction.
\textbf{(C3)} A policy evaluation with minimum detectable effects:
failure-selective QE halves generator calls and, on NQ, significantly
reduces unconditional QE's harm, but no policy detectably beats BM25.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Selective QE and query performance prediction.} Pre- and
post-retrieval QPP (Clarity~\cite{qpp-clarity},
WIG~\cite{wig}, NQC~\cite{nqc}) predicts query difficulty. Selective
QE goes further: Cronen-Townsend et al.~\cite{selective-qe} decide
whether to expand by comparing the clarity of the expanded and
unexpanded rankings, i.e.\ they predict expansion benefit directly;
Amati et al.~\cite{amati-selective} gate expansion on difficulty
predictors; Lv and Zhai~\cite{lv-zhai-adaptive} predict a per-query
feedback weight; and Collins-Thompson~\cite{collins-thompson-risk}
trades expansion reward against risk. These works evaluate the gated
system in aggregate; they do not report detection and benefit
prediction as two AUCs on the same queries, nor decompose the headroom
between them.

\textbf{LLM QE and its failures.} Query2doc~\cite{query2doc},
MuGI~\cite{mugi}, and CSQE~\cite{csqe} expand unconditionally. Weller
et al.~\cite{weller-qe-fails} find generative expansions often hurt
strong retrievers; Abe et al.~\cite{qe-fails-unfamiliar} trace
failures to unfamiliar or ambiguous queries. Kotte~\cite{not-all-queries-need-rewriting}
gates prompt-only rewriting for dense retrievers (gate AUC $0.593$,
no reliable gain over never rewriting); Adaptive-RAG~\cite{adaptive-rag}
routes on a question-complexity classifier. Neither separates the
gate's detection accuracy from its benefit prediction.

\textbf{Uplift modelling.} Predicting an outcome is not predicting a
treatment's effect on it~\cite{causal-classification}; targeting the
customers most likely to churn can be ineffective~\cite{ascarza-futility}.
Our setting has an advantage marketing lacks: both outcomes (expanded
and unexpanded) are observed for every query offline, so the uplift
label is exact up to relevance-label noise.

\section{Setup}

\textbf{Data.} TripClick~\cite{tripclick}: 1.52M health documents; the
TAIL query bucket's validation and test splits (1{,}175 queries each),
with the click-derived relevance labels TripClick releases. BEIR
NQ~\cite{beir,natural-questions}: 2.68M passages, human-annotated
relevance; we carve a fixed-seed split (800 validation) and use a
disjoint 2{,}052-query test set, run once under a scope committed
before any of its data was touched. An earlier $n{=}600$ NQ pilot is
reported only in the ledger below.

\textbf{Pipeline.} BM25 over SQLite FTS5 (Porter stemming, title
weight selected on validation). From pre-action telemetry a
deterministic rule picks one of four expansion operators or none; it
picks none for $1.5\%$ (TripClick) and $1.9\%$ (NQ) of queries. The
rest form the \emph{acted-upon} set, on which every AUC in
Table~\ref{tab:headline} is computed. The generator (\texttt{qwen2.5:3b},
4-bit, greedy, $\le 2$ calls per query) writes a corpus-grounded
expansion from the top retrieved evidence; the expanded query is
retrieved and, on TripClick and in both dense settings, reranked with
a MiniLM cross-encoder~\cite{minilm-cross-encoder} (the NQ BM25 run
does not rerank).
The dense setting replaces first-stage retrieval with
\texttt{bge-small-en-v1.5}~\cite{bge-embedding} over the full
collections.

\textbf{Labels.} \emph{Failure}: the first-stage top 10 contains no
relevant document (nDCG@10 $=0$); $45\%$ of TripClick and $49\%$ of
NQ test queries under BM25. \emph{Action utility}:
$\Delta = \text{nDCG@10(expanded)} - \text{nDCG@10(original)}$,
with \emph{harmed} ($\Delta<0$) as the positive class and ties
($\Delta=0$) counted as not harmed. Ties are $48\%$ (TripClick) and
$69\%$ (NQ) of acted-upon queries, and harm $33\%$ and $17\%$. Harm is
impossible on a failing query, since its nDCG is already $0$.

\textbf{Models and features.} Detection: logistic regression on 8
pre-action features (top score, score margin, score entropy,
query-term coverage, mean rare-term IDF, entity overlap, result
coherence, ranking stability under a small query perturbation); 6
under dense retrieval (no IDF, no stability). Action utility: 10
post-action features (gains in score margin, coverage and coherence;
stability; rewrite drift; reranker--first-stage disagreement; top-$k$
overlap with the original; reranker top score and margin; query-length
ratio), fit by linear regression on $\Delta$ under BM25 and as a
logistic harm classifier under dense retrieval (9 features, no
stability). Every model and threshold is fit on validation and scored
once on test; significance is a paired bootstrap over queries
($10^4$ resamples, seed 42).

\textbf{Policies.} \emph{BM25}: never expand. \emph{Static QE}: always
expand; TripClick deploys a reciprocal-rank fusion of the original and
expanded rankings (no fused ranking was stored for NQ, whose arms use
the expanded ranking itself). \emph{Failure-selective QE}: expand iff
$P(\text{fail}\mid x)>\tau$, $\tau$ the median validation prediction.
\emph{Closed-loop QE} (TripClick only): expand, then accept the fused
ranking, roll back, or re-plan once based on the action-utility score,
with validation-calibrated thresholds.

\textbf{BM25 baseline.} Our first, unstemmed BM25 scored nDCG@10
$0.228$ on TripClick TAIL test, below the published $0.267$~\cite{tripclick}.
Re-selected on validation only (validation $0.235\to0.327$), BM25 alone
(no reranker) scores $0.315$ on test.

\textbf{Test-access ledger.} TripClick TAIL test was used during
development before our data-use protocol existed: an early verifier and
its thresholds were calibrated on it, two apparatus defects were found
there (the reranked order was not wired into the accepted ranking;
the verifier judged a different ranking than the one accepted), and
the weak $0.228$ BM25 was observed there. We then froze the protocol,
refit every model and threshold on validation, checked BM25 once
($0.315$), and ran one frozen evaluation that produced every TripClick
test episode used here, plus a 150-query dense pilot and one full
dense run. All later analyses,
including this revision's, recombine those frozen records under
pre-specified rules without refitting on test. NQ test was touched
three times, each under a written pre-commitment: the $n{=}600$ pilot
(detection $0.638$ $[0.593,0.681]$, action utility $0.522$
$[0.442,0.602]$), the confirmatory $2{,}052$-query run, and its dense
rerun; two later BM25 re-runs on the same queries recomputed
telemetry and score lists only. Scripts and pre-commitments are in the anonymized repository:
\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/agentic-query-expansion-8939/}.

Under the pre-committed harm label, detection beats action utility on
the same queries in three of four settings (Table~\ref{tab:headline}).
On the full test populations, detection AUC is $0.649$, $0.680$,
$0.638$, and $0.688$. Dense TripClick is the exception; there the
bi-encoder is weaker than BM25 (nDCG@10 $0.217$ vs.\ $0.315$), while
on NQ it is stronger ($0.500$ vs.\ $0.308$).

\textbf{Detection is ordinary QPP.} The unsupervised NQC predictor over
the top-100 BM25 scores~\cite{nqc} (normalized by the top-$k$ mean, as
FTS5 exposes no corpus score) matches our detector on TripClick
($0.647$ vs.\ $0.649$; paired difference $+0.003$ $[-0.030,+0.035]$)
and beats it on NQ ($0.721$ vs.\ $0.680$; $-0.041$ $[-0.064,-0.017]$).
WIG~\cite{wig} is weaker ($0.588$ and $0.651$). Detection is not the novel part; it is what
selective QE already has.

\textbf{Not a feature-set artifact.} A logistic harm model on
pre-action \emph{plus} post-action features (18 under BM25, 15 dense)
still trails detection on TripClick BM25 ($0.603$; difference
$+0.040$ $[+0.003,+0.078]$) and dense NQ ($0.625$; $+0.070$
$[+0.033,+0.107]$), and does not differ from it on dense TripClick
($0.609$; $+0.012$ $[-0.034,+0.058]$). Adding pre-action features
helps on dense NQ ($+0.028$ $[+0.010,+0.045]$ over post-action alone)
but not on TripClick (BM25 $+0.020$ $[-0.013,+0.053]$; dense $+0.008$).
NQ BM25 is missing: its test run stored no post-action features. A
logistic refit of the BM25 action-utility model gives $0.563$
($+0.011$ $[-0.008,+0.029]$ over the linear one).

\textbf{Not label sparsity.} Most TAIL queries have one relevant
document, so failure might track label sparsity. The relevant-document
count alone predicts failure at only $0.570$ (TripClick) and $0.521$
(NQ). Within count strata, detection AUC is $0.644$ and $0.677$
(TripClick; size-weighted $0.656$) and $0.687$ and $0.659$ (NQ), and
the detector stays significant when the count is controlled for.

\textbf{The gap is largely structural.} Expansion rarely rescues a
failing query: $84\%$, $93\%$, $94\%$, and $84\%$ of acted-upon failing
queries keep nDCG@10 $=0$ (ties). Failing queries are therefore mostly
``not harmed'', so the two labels are coupled, and the harm AUC
compares a detector against a label that is itself largely a failure
indicator. Dropping the failing queries, where harm is impossible,
gives harm AUCs of $0.553$, $0.582$, $0.627$, and $0.655$, above the
full-set values in three of four settings: the tied failing queries
pull the harm AUC down. The dense
harm score detects change rather than direction: it ranks
\emph{helped} queries as likely harmed (helped-vs.-rest AUC $0.464$
and $0.406$, against $0.540$ and $0.599$ under BM25).
Restricted to queries whose nDCG the expansion changed (last
two columns), the BM25 gaps vanish (point estimates reverse, CIs span
zero) because detection itself drops to $0.53$--$0.58$ there; only
dense NQ keeps a significant gap ($+0.122$). Predicting the
\emph{direction} of a change is weak everywhere ($0.51$--$0.62$). It
is better for large effects: on the half of changed queries with the
largest $|\Delta|$, it reaches $0.644$ and $0.711$ under BM25, though
only $0.551$ and $0.526$ under dense retrieval. Under BM25, the
action-utility score's errors are therefore concentrated in small
changes.

\textbf{Policies.} On TripClick, no policy differs from BM25 or from
any other; every pairwise CI spans zero (Table~\ref{tab:policies}).
These nulls are bounded: with MDEs of $0.007$--$0.011$ nDCG@10, effects
smaller than that could exist undetected. On NQ, where the expanded
ranking is used unfused, unconditional QE significantly harms
($-0.022$). Failure-selective QE recovers most of that loss
($+0.015$ $[+0.008,+0.022]$ over Static QE); its residual $-0.007$
against BM25 has a CI that just reaches zero. Failure-selective QE routes $49\%$ (TripClick) and $51\%$
(NQ) of queries to the generator, halving calls relative to Static QE.
The closed-loop agent costs $24\%$ more calls (1.22 vs.\ 0.98 per
query; Static QE skips the $1.5\%$ of queries the planner leaves
unexpanded) and $36\%$ more latency than Static QE, for no measurable
gain.

\textbf{Headroom decomposition.} The oracle selector expands a query
iff that helps; it is non-negative by construction and is an upper
bound, not a significance result. A \emph{perfect detector} expands
exactly the failing queries. Because QE can only help a failing query
(TripClick $+0.023$ on failing vs.\ $-0.018$ on healthy queries; NQ
$+0.028$ vs.\ $-0.069$), perfect detection is safe, but it recovers
only $42\%$ (TripClick) and $33\%$ (NQ) of the oracle's gain: most
failing queries are not rescued. The remaining $58\%$ and $67\%$ lies in
healthy queries that expansion sometimes helps and sometimes harms,
which only an action-utility predictor can separate. Our detector
captures part of the first share: its routed queries gain $+0.0055$
$[-0.005,+0.016]$ on TripClick, against $-0.0040$ for the rest.

\textbf{Heterogeneity is real; predicting it depends on the retriever.} Ranking queries
by the action-utility score captures at most $12\%$ of the oracle's
gain (Figure~\ref{fig:quota}). Yet the score is not noise. In a
best-linear-predictor test~\cite{gates} of $\Delta$ on the
validation-fit score under BM25, the slope is $+1.09$ $[+0.54,+1.67]$ on
TripClick and $+1.21$ $[+0.96,+1.44]$ on NQ, and the top score quartile's mean
$\Delta$ exceeds the bottom quartile's by $+0.093$ and $+0.120$ (both
CIs exclude zero). Under dense retrieval the evidence is weaker:
the slope is $+0.42$ $[+0.14,+0.71]$ on TripClick and $+0.11$
$[-0.01,+0.24]$ on NQ, not significant. Treatment effects vary across
queries, and post-action telemetry tracks them weakly, more so under
BM25. The oracle's magnitude is
split-sensitive on TripClick (validation $+0.053$ $[0.045,0.062]$ vs.\
test $+0.025$ $[0.020,0.030]$) but not on NQ ($+0.040$ $[0.031,0.049]$
vs.\ $+0.042$ $[0.036,0.047]$), so we read it as an order of magnitude,
not a bound to target.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

\textbf{Why action utility is harder.} Both outcomes are observed
offline, so the difficulty is not counterfactual missingness. Detection
describes a ranking that already exists. Action utility depends on
what a generator writes from evidence it did not choose; the output is
deterministic given its input (greedy decoding) but varies sharply
across queries, and its effect on a single relevant document's rank is
small and discrete. That fits the evidence above: direction is
predictable for large changes and close to chance for small ones.

\textbf{Limitations.} One small (3B) generator under a two-call
budget; a stronger one may change both the rescue rate and the policy
results. Two corpora; under dense retrieval, retriever strength and
domain are confounded. TripClick's relevance labels are click-derived
and sparse, which is one reason we report NQ's human labels in
parallel. The policy comparisons on NQ use unfused rankings and lack a
closed-loop arm. Of the analyses above, only the paired difference on
changed queries (Table~\ref{tab:headline}, last column) was not
pre-committed; every pre-committed analysis, including those
summarized here in one line, is reported in full in the repository (\texttt{docs/revision\_analyses\_summary.json}).

\section{Conclusion}

Detecting that retrieval has failed is ordinary QPP; under BM25 our
detector is no better than NQC. Knowing that an expansion will fix it is a separate
problem. Expansion rarely rescues a failing query, so even perfect
failure detection recovers only a third to two-fifths of selective
QE's headroom. The rest requires predicting whether a specific rewrite
helps a query that is already working, which our telemetry does only
weakly. In practice, failure-selective QE halves generator cost and
avoids most of unconditional QE's harm, but it does not by itself
deliver gains over BM25. Selective-QE work should therefore report
action utility on the same queries as detection, together with its
tie rule, rather than treating a failure detector as a proxy for
benefit.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

All data used (TripClick, BEIR Natural Questions) are existing research
benchmarks, used under their licenses; TripClick is used for
non-commercial research, and no model or per-query output fit on it is
released. TripClick's click logs are aggregate, de-identified
interactions, and we make no attempt to re-identify searchers. No
human-subjects data was collected. The generator is a small, locally
hosted model used only to rewrite queries within a bounded action
space. Our central finding cautions against deploying generative
interventions on the strength of a failure detector alone.
