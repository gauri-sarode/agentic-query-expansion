\title{Query Performance Prediction Is Not Benefit Prediction: Observability for Selective Retrieval Interventions}

\author{Anonymous Author(s)}
\affiliation{\institution{Affiliation withheld for double-blind review}}

\begin{abstract}
Query performance prediction (QPP) estimates whether a ranking is
poor, but selective retrieval needs a different prediction: whether an
intervention will improve it. We formalize this distinction as a
decision under observable telemetry and derive an exact, cost-aware
decomposition of the gain that no policy intervening only on failing
queries can reach. We evaluate LLM query expansion, cross-encoder
reranking, and pseudo-relevance feedback on six benchmarks, four of
them pre-registered and untouched. Without a budget cap, a perfect
failure gate recovers only $8$--$57\%$ of the oracle's gain. On the
same queries, the learned failure gate's score ranks failing queries
far better than queries the intervention helps (pre-registered on three
untouched test sets: AUC $0.72$--$0.89$ against $0.43$--$0.64$). For
fixed-candidate reranking, rescuing a failing query requires a
judged-relevant document below the top ten by construction; yet the
failure score often ranks such repairable failures below unrepairable
ones (AUC $0.27$--$0.53$; $0.37$ and $0.40$ pre-registered). The
routing value of failure scores varies by corpus: clear on HotpotQA,
against our pre-registered prediction, though computing the gate there
costs more than reranking every query; small or uncertain elsewhere.
Stronger pre-action predictors and partial probes help inconsistently.
Selective retrieval should be evaluated by intervention benefit,
routing gain, and computation cost, not by failure-detection accuracy
alone.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Retrieval interventions help some queries and harm others. LLM query
expansion (QE) can rewrite a query using the evidence it
retrieves~\cite{csqe}, yet it harms a measurable fraction of
queries~\cite{weller-qe-fails,qe-fails-unfamiliar}; cross-encoder
reranking and pseudo-relevance feedback (PRF) are equally uneven. The
natural response is to intervene selectively, and the natural gate is
a query performance predictor (QPP): intervene when the current
ranking looks like it is failing~\cite{amati-selective,adaptive-rag}.
That the gate's target differs from the quantity a policy needs is
not new: selective-QE work predicts expansion benefit
directly~\cite{selective-qe}, and uplift modelling shows that
targeting high-risk cases is not targeting the cases a treatment
helps~\cite{ascarza-futility,causal-classification}. What is missing
is how much a failure gate gives up, why, and whether the telemetry a
deployed system can observe, its \emph{observability}, can do better.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\textbf{(C1)} A decision framing of selective intervention under
observable telemetry, with an exact decomposition of the gain no
policy acting only on failing queries can capture, at zero and
positive cost (Section~\ref{sec:framework}).
\textbf{(C2)} Evidence on six corpora, four pre-registered and
untouched, that the targets differ and that the difference has
measurable consequences: the uncapped \emph{failure-only ceiling} is
$8$--$57\%$ of the oracle's gain, and failure scores rank helped
queries far below how they rank failing ones.
\textbf{(C3)} A structural constraint, reachability, with evidence
that failure scores often rank repairable failures below unrepairable
ones, and pre-registered tests showing that the routing value of
failure scores, stronger predictors, and probes varies by corpus.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{QPP and selective QE.} Pre- and post-retrieval QPP
(Clarity~\cite{qpp-clarity}, WIG~\cite{wig}, NQC~\cite{nqc}) predicts
query difficulty. Cronen-Townsend et al.~\cite{selective-qe} compare
the clarity of expanded and unexpanded rankings; Amati
et al.~\cite{amati-selective} gate expansion on difficulty predictors;
Lv and Zhai~\cite{lv-zhai-adaptive} predict a feedback weight;
Collins-Thompson~\cite{collins-thompson-risk} trades reward against risk. Even idealised selective
QE needs QPP at Kendall $\tau\ge0.5$~\cite{hauff-qpp-effective}, and
Chifu et al.~\cite{chifu-qpp-limits} find QPP accuracy strongly
collection-dependent and QPP-driven selective processing only
marginally better. To our knowledge, no such work bounds what a
failure-restricted policy can recover, scores failure and benefit on
the same queries, or explains the gate's selection error.

\textbf{LLM QE and adaptive retrieval.} Query2doc~\cite{query2doc},
MuGI~\cite{mugi}, and CSQE~\cite{csqe} expand unconditionally;
generative expansions often hurt strong retrievers~\cite{weller-qe-fails}
and fail on unfamiliar or ambiguous queries~\cite{qe-fails-unfamiliar}.
Kotte~\cite{not-all-queries-need-rewriting} gates prompt-only rewriting
for dense retrievers without reliable gain. Sarode~\cite{sarode-cods}
finds that a learned selector over candidate LLM rewrites captures little
oracle headroom and transfers inconsistently; we ask whether to intervene
at all, across three interventions, and bound failure-restricted policies.
Adaptive-RAG~\cite{adaptive-rag} routes on question complexity and
Mallen et al.~\cite{mallen-adaptive} retrieve only for less popular
entities. Graph-based adaptive reranking~\cite{gar} extends the
candidate pool beyond first-stage retrieval; we instead ask whether
failure scores identify which queries a fixed-pool intervention can
help. Cascade rankers~\cite{cascade-ranking} spend costly stages
only on promising candidates, and cutoff and efficiency
predictors~\cite{culpepper-cutoff,tonellotto-selective} set per-query
reranking depth or pruning. In uplift terms~\cite{causal-classification}, both
outcomes are observed offline, so our label is exact up to relevance
noise.

\section{Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

For a query $q$, let $R_0$ be the original ranking and $R_a$ the
ranking after intervention $a$, with metric $M$ (nDCG@10). The
intervention's effect is $\Delta_a(q)=M(R_a,q)-M(R_0,q)$ and its cost
$c_a$ ($\Delta_0=c_0=0$). With full information the best decision is
$a^*(q)=\arg\max_a\{\Delta_a(q)-\kappa c_a\}$, for a cost weight
$\kappa$. A deployed controller sees only telemetry $Z$, so the best
it can do is $\pi^*(Z)=\arg\max_a\{\mathbb{E}[\Delta_a\mid Z]-\kappa c_a\}$.
What matters is therefore what $Z$ reveals about $\Delta_a$, not about
$M(R_0)$. Two information sets differ in cost: \emph{pre-action}
telemetry $Z_0$ describes $R_0$ and costs no intervention, though
computing it is not free; \emph{post-action}
telemetry $Z_1$ also describes $R_a$ and exists only after $c_a$ is
paid. A \emph{probe} observes part of the intervention, such as
cross-encoder scores of a few candidates, at part of its cost
(Section~\ref{sec:probe}). A failure gate replaces $\mathbb{E}[\Delta_a\mid Z_0]$ with
$P(F\mid Z_0)$, where $F=\mathbf{1}[M(R_0,q)=0]$.

\textbf{The failure-only ceiling.} Fix one intervention with $c_a=1$,
so $\kappa$ is its cost per query in nDCG@10 units. The oracle selector earns
$G_{\text{orc}}(\kappa)=\mathbb{E}[(\Delta-\kappa)_+]$. A policy that
may act only on failing queries does best by intervening where $F=1$
and $\Delta>\kappa$, earning $G^*_{F}(\kappa)=\mathbb{E}[F(\Delta-\kappa)_+]$
(the \emph{failure-restricted oracle}; its share of $G_{\text{orc}}$ is
the failure-only ceiling), so
\[
G_{\text{orc}}(\kappa)-G^*_{F}(\kappa)
=\mathbb{E}[(1-F)(\Delta-\kappa)_+]
\]
(gain on healthy queries) bounds every failure-restricted policy at any
$\kappa\ge0$. Because $M\ge 0$, a failing query cannot be harmed
($\Delta\ge 0$ when $F=1$), so at $\kappa=0$ a \emph{perfect} failure
gate, intervening iff $F=1$, attains $G^*_F$. At $\kappa>0$ it earns
$G_F(\kappa)=\mathbb{E}[F(\Delta-\kappa)]$, a further
$\mathbb{E}[F(\kappa-\Delta)_+]$ below $G^*_F$ spent on failing queries
helped by less than $\kappa$, which only information beyond the binary
label avoids. The identity is elementary; its use is that every term is
measurable offline for any intervention. It does not bound a policy
built from a failure \emph{score} that also acts on healthy queries.

\textbf{Information vs.\ policy regret.} For a policy $\pi(S)$ on a
score $S$ at budget $b$, $V_{\text{orc}}-V_\pi=(V_{\text{orc}}-V_S^*)+(V_S^*-V_\pi)$,
with $V_S^*$ the best budget-feasible policy using $S$ alone: lost
information plus misuse. We bracket $V_S^*$ by cross-fitted and
optimistic in-sample ten-bin policies (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\section{Setup}

\textbf{Data.} TripClick~\cite{tripclick}: 1.52M health documents, the
TAIL bucket's validation and test splits (1{,}175 queries each), with
click-derived relevance labels. BEIR NQ~\cite{beir,natural-questions}:
2.68M passages, human labels; 800 validation and a disjoint
2{,}052-query test set. BEIR ArguAna~\cite{arguana}: 8{,}674
arguments, 1{,}406 queries whose relevant document is the
counterargument; BEIR FiQA~\cite{fiqa}: 57{,}638 financial posts;
BEIR DBPedia-Entity~\cite{dbpedia-entity}: 4.63M entities, 400 test
queries with graded labels; BEIR HotpotQA~\cite{hotpotqa}: 5.23M
passages, 7{,}405 multi-hop test questions (Section~\ref{sec:arguana}).

\textbf{Interventions.} \emph{LLM QE}: BM25 over SQLite FTS5; a
deterministic rule picks one of four expansion operators or none, and \texttt{qwen2.5:3b} (4-bit,
greedy, $\le2$ calls) writes a corpus-grounded expansion. On TripClick
and under dense retrieval (\texttt{bge-small-en-v1.5}~\cite{bge-embedding})
the expanded ranking's top candidates are reranked by a MiniLM
cross-encoder~\cite{minilm-cross-encoder}; the original is not, so
those labels include the reranker's effect. TripClick's deployed QE
fuses original and expanded rankings (RRF); NQ's uses the expanded
ranking. \emph{Reranking}: the cross-encoder scores the BM25 top 100;
its top 50 lead. \emph{PRF}: RM3-style~\cite{relevance-models}
weights from the top $d$ documents select $t$ terms whose BM25 ranking
is interpolated with the original (weight $\lambda$); $(d,t,\lambda)$
is tuned on validation over 108 settings.

\textbf{Labels and models.} \emph{Failure}: $F=1$ ($45\%$ of TripClick
and $49\%$ of NQ test queries under BM25). \emph{Harmed}: $\Delta<0$,
ties not harmed. Detection is a logistic regression on 8 pre-action
features (score shape, term coverage, rare-term IDF, entity overlap,
coherence, perturbation stability; 6 under dense). Action utility is
fit on $\Delta$ from post-action features. For
pre-action routing, a \emph{benefit} model regresses $\Delta$ on the 8
pre-action features alone. Three further benefit predictors,
pre-registered for ArguAna (Section~\ref{sec:arguana}), use 10
pre-action features (the 8 above, NQC, query length), as does the
failure gate on ArguAna and FiQA: B1, LightGBM regression on $\Delta$;
B3, LightGBM quantile regression; and B2, the distance-weighted mean
$\Delta$ of the $k$ nearest training queries under frozen
\texttt{bge-small-en-v1.5} embeddings. Every model and threshold is fit on
validation and scored once on test; intervals are paired bootstraps
over queries ($10^4$ resamples). We call the deployed detector,
used to route, the \emph{learned failure gate}, and any model of
$\Delta$ a \emph{benefit predictor}.

\textbf{Evidence ledger.} Only the ArguAna, FiQA, DBPedia, and
HotpotQA results (Section~\ref{sec:arguana}) are independent
confirmation: every NQ
query and the TripClick TAIL test set have been inspected.
\emph{Fixed before a test run}: the NQ, reranking, and PRF runs.
\emph{Pre-committed analyses of inspected traces}: the detection and harm AUCs, ceiling,
budget routing, and Holm correction. \emph{Exploratory}: TripClick test was used during
development, the changed-query harm comparisons were added after
seeing the main results, and the
NQ B1/B2 runs, diagnostics, probes of Section~\ref{sec:probe}, and the
reachability analysis on the first four corpora
followed protocols frozen before computing but after the test sets
were inspected; analyses added after review are marked in the
text.
Pre-commitments and scripts are in the anonymized repository:
\url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/agentic-query-expansion-8939/}.

\textbf{Failure-restricted policies miss most of the gain.} The
failure-restricted oracle captures $8$--$57\%$ of the oracle's gain
(Table~\ref{tab:ceiling}; $18$--$46\%$ on ArguAna and FiQA), though it
never makes a query worse; the lowest shares, and the widest intervals,
come from PRF, which barely acts. The share follows how often an
intervention rescues a failing query relative to how often it helps a
healthy one: reranking rescues $17$--$46\%$ of failing queries, so up
to half its headroom lies there; LLM QE rescues $6$--$15\%$ and PRF
almost never. The rest sits in healthy queries the intervention also
often harms, which only benefit prediction can separate. At a
per-query cost of $0.02$ the shares are similar ($8$--$57\%$;
$22$--$47\%$ on ArguAna and FiQA), and intervening on every failing
query then loses outright for PRF and dense TripClick QE.

\textbf{A budget cap loosens the restriction only where rescues are
common} (exploratory, post-review). At a $10\%$ cap, failing queries
hold $54$--$98\%$ of the capped oracle's gain for reranking and
ArguAna QE ($83\%$ on FiQA); the other settings stay near their
uncapped shares.

\textbf{The learned failure gate also selects poorly.} At a $10\%$
budget, $35$--$80\%$ of its picks are failing queries (the rest are healthy),
and it realises at most $34\%$ of the capped failure-restricted oracle's gain, and its
gain is negative in five of the first eleven settings (all three on
ArguAna) and in $7$--$9$ of them at $25$--$75\%$. Among failing queries its score ranks
the rescued ones no better than chance (Table~\ref{tab:auc}; below it in four settings, not on FiQA).

The ceiling and the selection error are separate constraints, and
both operate at every budget below $100\%$.

\textbf{The failure gate detects failure, not benefit.}
The learned failure gate ranks failing queries at AUC
$0.64$--$0.69$ (Table~\ref{tab:auc}), ordinary QPP accuracy: NQC~\cite{nqc}
reaches $0.647$ on TripClick and $0.721$ on NQ. Scored against whether the
intervention helps (exploratory, post-review), the same scores reach
only $0.42$--$0.56$, with intervals below $0.5$ for NQ PRF and
ArguAna QE and reranking and above only for dense NQ QE.
Pre-registered on FiQA, DBPedia, and HotpotQA, the gap replicates
in every setting (Holm $p<0.001$; Section~\ref{sec:arguana}), and
Figure~\ref{fig:profile} shows its shape.

\textbf{Benefit predictors do better but are weak}
(helped AUC $\le0.58$; NQ probe $0.65$, HotpotQA B1 $0.67$ and probe $0.73$). They beat the failure score
on NQ reranking ($+0.08$ to $+0.17$), NQ PRF, and ArguAna QE and
reranking ($+0.07$ to $+0.12$); elsewhere differences mostly span zero.


\textbf{Why: reachability} (exploratory on the first four corpora,
protocol frozen before computing; pre-registered on DBPedia and
HotpotQA). For fixed-candidate reranking, rescuing a failing query
requires a judged-relevant document at BM25 ranks 11--100, by
construction. Empirically, such \emph{reachable} failures vary widely
($51$--$83\%$ of failing queries), $29$--$88\%$ of them are rescued,
yet the failure score often ranks reachable failures below
unreachable ones (AUC $0.27$--$0.53$; $0.40$ $[0.27,0.54]$ and $0.37$
$[0.33,0.41]$ pre-registered), and among reachable failures the helped
rate shows no consistent trend across its tertiles. The highest
failure scores can thus prioritise queries this reranker cannot
repair. Among healthy queries, harm concentrates where the first
relevant document is already at rank 1 (mean $\Delta$ $-0.02$ to
$-0.35$ on the first four corpora).

\section{Routing at Matched Budgets}
\label{sec:routing}






\textbf{Pre-action routing.} At every budget ($10$--$75\%$), each
scorer that decides before intervening stays close to random routing
and far below the oracle on TripClick, NQ, and ArguAna (table and
cost curves in the repository). Failure routing beats
random by at most $0.006$ at any budget and often falls below it.
Averaged over budgets, NQC is
no better than the detector, and benefit routing beats failure
routing only for reranking on NQ ($+0.009$ $[+0.003,+0.015]$), where
it leads at every budget (per-budget CIs in the repository); B1 and B2 do no better
(exploratory).

\section{Pre-Registered Replications}
\label{sec:arguana}


\textbf{ArguAna.} \emph{Question:} do stronger pre-action benefit
predictors beat failure routing on data never inspected for this work?
\emph{Design}, pre-registered before download
(\texttt{configs/\allowbreak arguana\_\allowbreak precommit.json}): a
debate-grouped split ($701/275/430$), the three interventions (LLM QE
as on NQ), and Holm-corrected primary contrasts for B1--B3 (B3's
registered quantile choice is unsound, so a secondary B3 fixes
$0.25$); leakage fixes were dated amendments before any label was
read.
\emph{Result: no.} Every intervention loses on average (reranking
$-0.071$ $[-0.108,-0.034]$, LLM QE $-0.163$), yet the oracle could
gain $0.053$ and $0.010$ budget-averaged, so the task is pure
selection. For reranking, B1 minus failure routing is $+0.015$
$[-0.001,+0.028]$ (Holm $p=0.13$), B2 minus failure $+0.008$ (Holm
$p=0.37$), B3 minus B1 under a budget cap $+0.006$ ($p=0.52$); the best
recovers $16\%$ of the oracle-minus-failure headroom, below the
pre-registered $25\%$ bar, and a secondary family agrees (none of nine
survives Holm). Failure routing of reranking is below random at every
budget. PRF changes only $9\%$ of rankings,
so its rows are uninformative. At $430$ queries, B1's $+0.015$ may be
real but is unresolved.

\textbf{FiQA} (predictions fixed before download, one Holm family;
$1{,}500$/$500$/$648$ queries). \emph{Confirmed:} the failure score
ranks failure above benefit ($0.72$ against $0.52$ and $0.46$, Holm
$p<0.001$). \emph{Not confirmed:} the within-failing inversion ($0.56$
$[0.49,0.63]$) and Probe-10 beating failure and B1 routing ($-0.001$,
$+0.006$; Holm $p\ge0.25$); B1 and B2 do not beat failure routing
either ($-0.006$ each).

\textbf{DBPedia and HotpotQA.} \emph{Question:} on new data, does the
failure score carry routing information, and is any shortfall
specific to QPP? \emph{Predictions}, fixed before download
(\texttt{configs/\allowbreak info\_\allowbreak value\_\allowbreak precommit.json}),
were built to let the account `pre-action scores carry little routing
information' fail: it fails if routing on S beats random (P2) or B1
beats S (P3); reachability fails if S separates reachable from
unreachable failing queries (P5). DBPedia's $400$ test queries are
scored by five-fold cross-fitting; HotpotQA uses $1{,}500$ training and
$500$ dev queries. \emph{Result} (Table~\ref{tab:iv}): the
detection--benefit gap (P1) and reachability (P5) hold on both.
DBPedia behaves as predicted, except that the probe does not help. On
HotpotQA, against the prediction, S beats random by $+0.023$, and B1
($+0.003$, small) and the probe beat S (Holm $p<0.001$); simply
ranking by S gives the same $+0.024$ (exploratory). There, reranking
helps more the higher the failure score (Figure~\ref{fig:profile}),
though not within failing queries (P5). By a Shapley split of the rise
from the lowest to the highest score tertile ($+0.17$), most comes from
larger gains on healthy queries ($+0.12$ $[0.11,0.13]$) and more
failures ($+0.06$), with rescue gain ($+0.01$) and reachability
($-0.01$) minor; on NQ, falling reachability ($-0.08$) cancels the
rise in failures (descriptive); HotpotQA's two-passage
judgments may contribute, untested. The gate's telemetry costs $2.8$\,s
per query beyond the shared BM25 retrieval, reranking $0.22$\,s, so
reranking every query is cheaper; but a gate built from the BM25 score
list and query length, at negligible extra cost, routes as well ($+0.025$ over
random) and keeps $75\%$ of always reranking's gain at half its cost
(exploratory). PRF barely acts (mean $\Delta$ $\pm0.003$).

\section{Partial Observation and Cost}
\label{sec:probe}



A probe sits between pre- and post-action telemetry. In an exploratory
analysis (protocol frozen before computing), the cross-encoder scores
the first $10$ BM25 candidates; B1's model regresses full reranking's
$\Delta$ on nine probe features, and the top-scoring queries are fully
reranked. Each policy is charged its median latency on 50 validation
queries: full reranking $0.48$, $1.11$, $1.26$, $0.84$\,s on NQ,
TripClick, ArguAna, FiQA; the probe $0.05$--$0.13$\,s.

On NQ, where reranking usually helps, the Probe-10 policy beats
failure routing by $+0.024$ $[+0.017,$ $+0.031]$ and B1 by $+0.017$
$[+0.011,$ $+0.022]$ over $10$--$75\%$ reranked (both Holm-significant),
and its predictions rank $\Delta$ best (Spearman $0.25$). NQ's
pre-action telemetry (a perturbation search over $2.68$M passages)
takes longer than reranking every query, so always reranking dominates
failure and B1 routing; against free NQC (post-review), probe routing
gains $+0.020$ $[+0.012,+0.028]$ at equal median-latency cost. Probes
do not help on TripClick and ArguAna, where reranking loses on
average, nor on FiQA or DBPedia; they do on HotpotQA
(Section~\ref{sec:arguana}).

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}

QPP and selective retrieval predict different targets, and the
difference matters. One consequence is proven: at any cost,
failure-restricted policies are capped by the oracle gain on failing
queries ($8$--$57\%$ uncapped). The other is empirical, replicated on
three untouched corpora: failure scores rank helped queries far below
failing ones, and for reranking they often rank repairable failures
last. Yet failure routing has heterogeneous value: raw routing on the
failure score beats random clearly on HotpotQA ($+0.024$), slightly on
FiQA ($+0.008$ $[+0.002,+0.013]$), and not detectably on DBPedia
($+0.002$ $[-0.004,+0.007]$; all exploratory), whose $400$ queries
cannot exclude an effect a third of HotpotQA's. High QPP accuracy
neither guarantees nor precludes intervention value; benefit alignment
and the cost of the signal decide. Most of what failure routing
loses is information, not policy: even the optimistic in-sample
ten-bin policy on $S$ leaves $65$--$93\%$ of the oracle's lead over
random, and the cross-fitted one gains little over ranking by $S$
(except by abstaining on ArguAna). This limits the evaluated score
partition, not everything $S$ could encode. Reachability is predictable
from deep BM25 scores (AUC $0.59$--$0.70$ on five corpora), yet a
reachability-factored benefit model does not beat a matched single
model on any corpus (Holm $p=1.0$; all exploratory).

\textbf{Limitations.} One small (3B) generator; reranking and PRF only
under BM25, with one cross-encoder and one PRF family. Six corpora,
four untouched; three ($430$, $648$, $400$ test queries) are
underpowered for differences below about $0.015$;
under dense retrieval, retriever strength and domain are confounded,
and the dense QE labels include the reranker. TripClick's labels are
click-derived and sparse. The cost weight $\kappa$ is illustrative, latencies are single-machine (median, P95 check), and probes cover reranking only. All pre-committed results are reported.

\section{Conclusion}

QPP predicts whether a ranking is poor; selective retrieval needs to
know whether an intervention helps. We bound the gain failure-restricted
policies cannot reach and show that learned failure scores often
misrank intervention opportunities. For fixed-pool reranking,
reachability explains part of the mismatch: severe failures can be
impossible to rescue. The value of failure-based routing still varies
by corpus, as the pre-registered HotpotQA result shows. Selective
retrieval should be evaluated by intervention benefit, routing gain,
and computation cost, not by failure-detection accuracy alone.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

All data (TripClick and BEIR NQ, ArguAna, FiQA, DBPedia-Entity, and HotpotQA) are existing
research benchmarks used under their licenses; TripClick is used for
non-commercial research, and no model or per-query output fit on it is
released. TripClick's click logs are aggregate, de-identified
interactions, and we make no attempt to re-identify searchers. No
human-subjects data was collected. The generator is a small, locally
hosted model used only to rewrite queries within a bounded action
space. Our central finding cautions against deploying generative
interventions on the strength of a failure detector alone.
